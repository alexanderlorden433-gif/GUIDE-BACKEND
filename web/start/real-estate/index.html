Lesson 1 of 49 · 3 min read

Get licensed and choose your first brokerage

Before you buy a prep course or book an exam date, find out exactly what your area requires. Real estate licensing rules are set locally, so the course hours, the exam, any background check and the continuing education you'll need after you pass all depend on where you plan to work. Your state, provincial or national real estate regulator publishes the official requirements, and that's the source to trust over any ad for a course.

Once you're licensed, you'll usually need to work under a sponsoring brokerage, at least at first. This choice shapes your first year more than most new agents expect, and the brand on the sign is the least important part of it.

Compare brokerages on what they actually give you:

  • Training, and a real mentor you can call with questions
  • Help with leads, or at least with lead generation tools
  • The commission split you'll start on and how it improves
  • Monthly desk, technology or transaction fees

In your first year, strong mentorship usually beats a slightly better split. A great split pays you nothing until you close a deal, and the fastest way to your first closing is someone experienced walking you through it. Ask each brokerage who would actually train you, how often you'd meet, and whether new agents can shadow a showing or a listing appointment.

Interview at least three brokerages before you sign anything. Ask to speak with an agent who joined in the last year or two, and ask what support they really got. Their answer will tell you more than the recruiting pitch.

Your step

Find your local real estate regulator's website and write down the course hours, exam steps and renewal rules that apply to you.

Lesson 2 of 49 · 1 min read

Keep your license and classes up to date

A license that lapses can stop you from earning commissions, and it may put the deals you're working on at risk. Renewal dates and continuing education deadlines are easy to forget when you're busy with clients, which is exactly when they tend to sneak up on you.

Put your renewal date and every course deadline in your calendar, with a reminder about three months ahead. Book your classes as soon as that reminder goes off, so you're never cramming hours into the last week. The hours and topics you need depend on where you're licensed, so check your regulator's site each renewal cycle in case the requirements have changed.

Your step

Add your license renewal date and continuing education deadline to your calendar, with a reminder three months before each.

Lesson 3 of 49 · 3 min read

Work out what you'll actually take home per deal

The commission percentage you hear quoted on a sale is not what lands in your bank account. On a typical sale, the total commission is shared between the agent representing the seller and the agent representing the buyer. Then each agent shares their portion with their brokerage. How agents get paid differs by place and has been changing in some markets, so ask your broker exactly how it works where you are.

Your brokerage split is the next big piece, and splits vary a lot. Some brokerages start newer agents at something like 50/50 and improve the split as you sell more, while others offer a higher split with less support. On top of the split, many brokerages charge desk fees, technology fees or a fee on every transaction.

To see your real number, take an example sale and walk it through:

  1. Start with the commission on your side of the deal
  2. Subtract your brokerage's share under your split
  3. Subtract any transaction fee
  4. Subtract your own costs for that deal, like marketing and driving
  5. Set aside money for taxes from what's left, and ask an accountant how much

The result is usually much smaller than the headline figure. That's fine, as long as you know it in advance.

Use that per-deal number to plan. If you know roughly what one closing nets you, you can work out how many closings a year it would take to cover your living costs and your monthly fees. That tells you how much lead generation you really need to do. It also helps you judge whether a brokerage with a lower split but better training is the better deal for you right now.

Your step

Run one example sale through your brokerage's split and fees, subtract your costs, and write down what you'd actually take home.

Lesson 4 of 49 · 1 min read

Track your real take-home on every closed deal

Gross commission is the number everyone talks about, but it isn't your pay. Brokerage splits, fees, marketing, driving and taxes can shrink a commission far more than you'd expect when you're starting out, and you won't see how much unless you write it down.

Keep a simple sheet with one row per closed deal. Start with the gross commission, then subtract your brokerage split, any transaction or desk fees, the marketing you paid for and your travel costs. The last column is your net. Move a share of each net into a separate account for taxes, and ask an accountant how big that share should be. After a few deals, you'll know your real average and can plan around it.

Your step

Create a sheet with columns for gross commission, split, fees, marketing, travel and net, and fill in your most recent deal.

Lesson 5 of 49 · 1 min read

Review your split and fees once a year

The brokerage terms that made sense when you were brand new can cost you a lot once you're closing more deals. A split that felt fair while you needed constant training might be expensive now that you mostly work on your own, and fees add up quietly over a year.

Once a year, before your agreement renews, add up everything you paid your brokerage: splits, desk fees, technology fees and any other charges. Then ask two other brokerages what you'd have paid there for the same deals, and what support you'd gain or give up. You don't have to switch. Often the comparison is enough to start an honest conversation with your current broker about better terms.

Your step

Set a yearly reminder for a month before your brokerage agreement renews to total what you paid in splits and fees.

Lesson 6 of 49 · 3 min read

Pick an investing strategy that fits your money and time

If you want to invest in property rather than help other people buy and sell it, your first decision is which strategy you're actually pursuing. The three most common ones work very differently.

Buy-and-hold rental investing means buying a property, renting it out and keeping it for the long term. You're aiming for rent that covers your costs, plus whatever the property gains in value over the years. It usually needs a down payment and cash reserves up front, and it comes with ongoing work: tenants, repairs and the landlord rules that apply where you buy.

House flipping means buying a property, improving it and selling it again, ideally within months. The profit, if there is one, arrives at the sale. That makes flips sensitive to renovation costs, delays and the state of the market at the moment you sell.

Wholesaling means putting a property under contract and then assigning that contract to another buyer for a fee, without ever owning the property yourself. It needs much less cash, but it depends almost entirely on your skill at finding good deals and on having buyers ready to take them. Rules on assigning contracts and marketing properties you don't own vary by place, and some areas regulate it closely, so check local rules before you try it.

Each strategy carries a different level of risk, a different cash requirement and a very different time commitment. Choose based on the money and hours you really have, not on which one looks most profitable in theory. A strategy you can afford to do carefully will teach you far more than one that stretches you thin.

Your step

Write down how much cash and how many hours a week you can truly commit, then circle the strategy that fits both.

Lesson 7 of 49 · 3 min read

Avoid the classic first-year mistakes

Most early mistakes in real estate come from not knowing the process yet, not from a lack of effort. The common ones are well known, which means you can plan around them before they cost you.

For new agents, the classic trap is the lead gap. Your first few deals often come from friends, family and former coworkers. Business feels like it's rolling, so you stop prospecting to focus on those clients. Then those deals close, everyone you know has already heard from you, and nothing new is in the pipeline. Because deals can take months from first contact to closing, the gap shows up long after the week you stopped. The fix is simple: keep doing some lead generation every week, especially when you're busy.

For new investors, the classic trap is the renovation budget. On a first flip or rehab, it's easy to price the work from a quick walkthrough and assume everything goes to plan. Then the contractor finds rotten subfloor, a permit takes longer than expected, and every extra month adds loan payments, insurance and utilities. A deal that looked solid can turn into a loss. Get real quotes, add a buffer for surprises and run your numbers on the slower timeline.

For both, the best protection is a mentor. Look for someone a few years ahead of you who has already made the mistakes you're about to make. That might be an experienced agent at your brokerage, a local investor you meet at a networking event, or a contractor who has worked on plenty of flips. Learning from their mistakes costs you a coffee and an hour. Repeating them yourself can cost you a deal, a client or your savings.

Your step

Write down two people a few years ahead of you in real estate and ask one of them for a short coffee chat this week.
