Lesson 1 of 49 · 3 min read

Build a portfolio for the clients you want

Your portfolio isn't there to prove you can do everything. Its job is to filter. It should make the clients you want think "this is for us" and quietly turn away the ones you don't. A page that jumps from a wedding invitation to a tech logo to a band poster makes it hard for anyone to picture you solving their problem.

Start by deciding what you want more of. Maybe it's brand identities for small food businesses, or packaging for skincare brands. Then pick only the projects that point in that direction and cut the rest, even pieces you're proud of. A small, focused set of strong work does more for you than a big mixed archive.

If you don't have paid client work yet, spec projects count. Redesign the identity of a real local business, or invent a brand for the kind of client you want and build it out properly. Label these clearly as personal or unsolicited projects. Done well, they show your skill and your thinking just as clearly as paid work, as long as nobody feels misled about what they're looking at.

For every piece, add a few lines of context under the images:

  • The challenge: what the client needed and why
  • Your solution: the key decisions you made
  • Where it's used: the sign, the packaging, the website

That short explanation is what separates you from a gallery of nice pictures. A potential client is hiring your thinking, and this is where they get to see it.

Review your portfolio every few months. As better projects come in, swap out the weakest piece so the whole thing keeps pointing at the work you actually want.

Your step

Write down the one type of client you want more of, then remove every portfolio piece that doesn't point toward them.

Lesson 2 of 49 · 1 min read

Turn your best project into a case study

Final images show that you can make something look good. A case study shows that you can solve a problem, and that's what clients are actually paying for. People hire designers they trust, and seeing your thinking builds that trust faster than another polished mockup.

Pick your strongest recent project and write it up as a short story. Cover the challenge the client brought you, a few early sketches or directions you dropped, the direction you chose and why, and what happened once it launched. Keep the writing short and the images plentiful. Add it to your portfolio this week, then do the same for each good project as it wraps up.

Your step

Pick your strongest recent project and write a short case study: the challenge, early sketches, the chosen direction and the result.

Lesson 3 of 49 · 1 min read

Keep your portfolio site fast and simple

It's tempting to build a heavy portfolio site with big animations and full-screen images. The trouble is that busy clients often look at your work on their phone, between meetings, on mobile data. If the page stalls, they close it before your best project ever appears.

Keep it simple. Compress your images, cut the site down to your six to eight best projects, and put the strongest one first. Then open it on your own phone with wifi turned off and click through every page. Anything that lags, jumps around or needs zooming to read gets fixed or removed.

Your step

Open your portfolio on your phone with wifi off, see how long your first project takes to appear, and fix anything that lags.

Lesson 4 of 49 · 3 min read

Develop a signature style without repeating yourself

A signature style is a real asset. When your portfolio shows a consistent point of view, like a certain way with color, a love of bold type or a habit of generous white space, it feels cohesive. Potential clients can quickly tell what working with you might look like, and the ones who like it reach out already half convinced.

The risk comes when that point of view turns into a formula. If every client gets the same rounded sans serif and the same muted palette, your work starts to look identical no matter who it was made for. That's a problem for the client, whose brand is supposed to stand apart, and for you, because your portfolio stops showing that you can think.

A useful check is to ask one question about each big decision: was this chosen because it serves this client's brand and audience, or because it's what you always do? A children's bookshop and a law firm might both deserve clean typography, but for very different reasons, and the results should feel different.

Your style should live in how you think and in the quality of your choices, not in a set of moves you copy from project to project. Keep the habits that keep solving problems well, and question the ones you reach for out of comfort.

One practical way to do this is to write down three things you want people to recognize in your work, such as clarity, warmth or a sharp sense of hierarchy. Those are qualities, not specific fonts or colors, so they can show up in very different brands without making them look alike.

Your step

Look at your last three projects and list any choice you made out of habit rather than for that client's brand or audience.

Lesson 5 of 49 · 1 min read

Watch design trends without chasing them

Knowing what's current keeps your work fresh and helps you talk to clients who ask for something modern. But a brand identity is meant to last, and trendy choices can look dated within a couple of years. Then your client is paying for a redesign much sooner than planned, and the work ages badly in your portfolio too.

Before using a trend in core branding, ask two questions: does it fit this client's audience, and will it still feel right in five years? If the answer is unclear, keep the logo and identity simple, and save the bolder trend for things meant to be short-lived, like social posts, campaigns or event graphics.

Your step

Name one trend you've been using lately and decide whether it belongs in core logos or only in short-lived social and campaign work.

Lesson 6 of 49 · 1 min read

Keep a swipe file of design you admire

A swipe file is a collection of design work you admire, organized so you can find it again. When a new brief lands, you're not starting from a blank page or scrolling for an hour. You have references ready, and you can show a client exactly what you mean by "warm but premium" instead of trying to describe it.

Whenever something catches your eye, like packaging in a shop, a website or a poster, screenshot or photograph it and drop it straight into a labeled folder. Organize by style and by industry, so "minimal", "playful" and "coffee shops" each have a home. At the start of each project, pull three or four pieces into a mood board as a starting point, not something to copy.

Your step

Create folders for three styles and three industries you like, and save five pieces of design you admire into them today.

Lesson 7 of 49 · 1 min read

Brand yourself the way you'd brand a client

Before a client sees your portfolio pieces, they see you: your website, your social profile, the proposal you send and the invoice that follows. If those all use different fonts and colors, it quietly undercuts your credibility. You're asking someone to trust you with their brand while yours looks improvised.

Treat yourself like a client. Pick two typefaces and a small palette, and write them down in a one-page style guide. Then update everything that carries your name to match: website, social profiles, proposal template, invoices and email signature. It doesn't need to be elaborate. It needs to be consistent.

Your step

Pick two typefaces and a small color palette for your own brand, and update your website and proposal template to match this week.

Lesson 8 of 49 · 1 min read

Separate business and personal money first

Before your first paid project, give the business its own bank account. When client payments and your grocery shopping run through the same account, taxes get messy, it's hard to tell whether the design work is actually making money, and paying you can feel less professional to clients.

Open a separate account just for design income and expenses, such as software subscriptions, fonts and equipment. Pay yourself from it on a set schedule, for example once a month, rather than dipping in whenever you need to. Rules about business accounts and taxes vary from place to place, so ask an accountant to help you set it up properly from the start.

Your step

Open a separate bank account for your design income and expenses, and book a short call with an accountant about setting it up.
