Lesson 1 of 49 · 3 min read

Choose your specialty and your ideal client

"Virtual assistant" can mean almost anything: inbox and calendar management, customer support, bookkeeping support, social media scheduling, project coordination. When your profile offers all of it, a busy business owner can't tell if you're the right fit, so they keep scrolling. A clear specialty fixes that, and it makes your pricing and marketing much easier too.

Start with what you already do well or genuinely like doing, not with whatever looks most in demand this month. Skills you enjoy are the ones you'll keep improving, and clients can feel the difference between someone who likes the work and someone putting up with it. Look back at past jobs, volunteer roles, even how you run your own household. If you're the person who keeps the family calendar and books every appointment, that's a real starting point.

Then pair the service with one type of client. A few examples of what that sounds like:

  • Inbox and calendar management for solo consultants
  • Customer support for small online shops
  • Bookkeeping support for local trades businesses
  • Podcast and social media scheduling for coaches

A pairing like this is easy to say and easy to pass on. When a friend hears "inbox and calendar help for consultants," a name pops into their head. "General admin help" doesn't bring anyone to mind. That's what makes a specific position so much easier to refer.

Choosing a specialty doesn't lock you in. You can still say yes to a good client outside it. Your specialty is simply what you lead with, and you can adjust it once you see which work you're best at and which clients you enjoy most.

Your step

Write one sentence that names the service you'll offer and the type of client you'll offer it to, like "inbox and calendar management for solo consultants."

Lesson 2 of 49 · 1 min read

Notice which tasks you enjoy most

The tasks you enjoy are often the ones you do fastest and best. That makes them a strong clue for your specialty, and specialists can often charge more and attract clients who suit them better than generalists do.

Keep the habit simple. At the end of each week, look at the tasks you logged and mark each one energizing, neutral or draining. If you don't have clients yet, do this with your current job, volunteer work or the admin you handle at home. After a month, a pattern shows up. Lead with the energizing work when you pitch new clients, and phase out the draining tasks where you can.

Your step

At the end of this week, mark every task you did as energizing, neutral or draining, and keep the list somewhere you'll see it.

Lesson 3 of 49 · 3 min read

Learn the tools VA clients expect you to know

Most clients won't ask whether you know a specific app. They'll assume you can handle the basics of running a small business online, and they'll notice quickly if you can't. The good news is that those basics fall into a few categories, and each category has one or two widely used options.

The categories to cover:

  • Email and calendar, including shared calendars and booking links
  • Project or task management boards
  • Team chat and video calls
  • Spreadsheets and shared documents

You don't need to master every product on the market. Get genuinely comfortable with the most widely used option in each category, and practice picking up a new one quickly. Set up a practice workspace and run through real tasks: build a project board, share a calendar, make a simple spreadsheet that tracks invoices. When a client uses something different, most of those skills carry over, and you can say honestly that you learn new tools fast.

Then list what you know clearly on your profile, website and proposals. A short line like "Comfortable in Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, Asana and Slack" lets a client check the fit without asking, and it signals that you're organized before you've even spoken.

Be honest about your level. Saying you're still learning a tool is far better than claiming expertise and fumbling through it in your first week with a client.

Your step

Pick the most common tool in each of the four categories, open a free account for any you haven't used, and complete one real task in each.

Lesson 4 of 49 · 3 min read

Price your work: hourly, retainer or package

There are three common ways to price VA work, and many VAs end up using more than one. The trick is matching the model to the kind of work, not picking one forever.

Hourly is the simplest. You track your time and bill for it. It's transparent, but it makes both sides a little anxious. Clients watch the hours add up, and you get penalized for being efficient: the faster you get, the less you earn for the same task. Hourly works best for one-off, unpredictable jobs, and for the first few weeks with a new client while you both figure out what the work involves.

A retainer is a set number of hours each month for a flat fee, usually paid in advance. Retainers are common in VA work because the work itself is ongoing. Inboxes and calendars never stop. Clients get a predictable cost and you get predictable income. The catch is that you have to track your hours against the block, or it's easy to quietly give away extra hours every month.

A package is a flat fee for a defined result, like "inbox cleanup and setup, plus one month of management." Packages suit bounded projects with a clear finish line, and they're a good way to start with a client who isn't ready for a retainer.

Say you offer a 20-hour monthly retainer, a setup package and an hourly rate for overflow work. That gives you a ready answer for most requests. Whichever models you choose, write down exactly what each one includes, so the client knows what they're paying for.

Your step

Write down your three offers: an hourly rate, one monthly retainer with its hours, and one package with a clear finish line.

Lesson 5 of 49 · 1 min read

Don't discount your rate to win a client

It's tempting to quote low to land your first retainer. The problem is that clients anchor on the first number they hear. When you later raise your rate to where it should be, the jump can feel unfair to them, even when your work fully justifies it.

Quote your real rate from the start. If the budget is tight, change what they get, not what each hour costs. Offer fewer hours, a shorter task list or a smaller starter package. For example: "My rate stays the same, but we could start with ten hours a month covering just your inbox, and add calendar work later." That keeps your price intact and gives the client a clear path to buy more.

Your step

Write down your real rate, then design a smaller starter package you can offer when a client says your price is too high.

Lesson 6 of 49 · 1 min read

Separate business and personal money now

When client payments land in the same account as your groceries and rent, tax time turns into hours of guesswork. Which deposits were income? Which purchases were business expenses? Separating the money before your first client pays you is one of the easiest bits of setup you'll ever do.

Open a dedicated account for the business and have every invoice paid into it. Pay business costs, like software subscriptions, from that account too. Move a set amount to your personal account each month as your pay. Each time a payment comes in, put part of it aside in a separate pot for taxes. How much depends on where you live and how you're set up, so ask an accountant or check your local tax office's guidance before you pick a number.

Your step

Open a separate account for your VA business this week, and update your invoice details so clients pay into it.
