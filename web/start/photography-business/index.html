Module 1: Find your niche and get shoot-ready

The whole first module, free. Each lesson ends with one thing to do, so you finish with real progress, not just notes.

Lesson 1 of 49 · 3 min read

Choose a niche instead of shooting everything

When you're new, saying yes to every kind of shoot feels like the fastest way to build a portfolio. It actually works against you. People find it hard to refer "a photographer who does everything," but they remember "the wedding photographer" or "the one who shoots product photos for small brands." A clear specialty is easy to pass along, and referrals are a big part of how photographers get booked.

Start with one or two niches. Look for the overlap between three things:

  • What you enjoy shooting enough to do it fifty times
  • What people near you actually pay for, like weddings, families, headshots or products
  • What your current photos already show you can do

To check local demand, search for your niche plus your town, like "newborn photographer Denver," and see who comes up. Finding a few busy competitors is a good sign, not a reason to stay away. It means people are paying for that kind of work.

Then make everything say the same thing. Your portfolio, website, social bio and the way you introduce yourself should all point at that niche. If someone lands on your page and can't tell what you shoot within a few seconds, the focus isn't clear yet.

You're not locked in forever. Once clients are coming in steadily, adding a related niche is easy, and wedding clients often come back for family photos later. Starting focused simply makes your early marketing work harder for you, because every post and every referral sends the same message.

Your step

Write down the one or two niches you'll focus on, then update your social bio so it names them in plain words.

Lesson 2 of 49 · 1 min read

Turn down jobs outside your niche when you can

Every off-niche shoot you take adds photos to your portfolio that you don't want more of, and it attracts more inquiries just like it. A few months of saying yes to everything and your page looks like a generalist's again.

When you can afford to, say no kindly and point the person to someone who does that work well. Keep a short decline message saved, something like: "Thanks so much for thinking of me. I focus on family and newborn sessions, so I'm not the best fit for your event, but I know a great event photographer and I'm happy to introduce you." Keep a list of two or three photographers in other niches you'd recommend. Referring out builds goodwill, and those photographers often send work back your way.

Your step

Write a two-line decline message and list two photographers in other niches you'd be happy to refer people to.

Lesson 3 of 49 · 3 min read

Build a portfolio that attracts your ideal client

A portfolio's job is to filter, not to prove you can shoot anything. When you show every image you've ever taken, your best work gets buried and potential clients can't tell what you actually specialize in. People book the photographer whose page looks like the photos they want.

Aim for 15 to 20 images. Choose each one with a single question: is this the kind of shoot I want to be booked for next? A striking street photo doesn't belong in a newborn portfolio, however proud you are of it.

A simple way to choose:

  1. Pull your 50 favorite images from your niche into one folder
  2. Cut anything with a technical flaw, like missed focus or blown-out highlights
  3. Cut near-repeats, keeping only the strongest of each kind of shot
  4. Ask a photographer friend to point out the weakest five, then remove them

If you shoot more than one niche, split the portfolio into clear categories, like Weddings and Portraits, so a couple planning a wedding doesn't have to scroll past headshots to find what they need.

Treat the portfolio as something that keeps changing. As your work improves, swap older, weaker images out instead of just adding new ones on top. A tight set of 18 strong photos says more about you than 80 mixed ones, and it brings in inquiries for exactly the work you showed.

Your step

Pick your 15 to 20 strongest niche images today and remove everything else from your portfolio page.

Lesson 4 of 49 · 1 min read

Review your portfolio every six months

Clients tend to remember the weakest photo on your page, not the best one. Old work also keeps attracting the kind of shoots you've moved past, so a portfolio you never review slowly starts working against you.

Put a recurring reminder in your calendar every six months. When it comes up, go through each image and ask one question: does this match the work I want booked next? If it doesn't, take it down, even if the client loved it at the time. Swap in a recent favorite only if it's genuinely stronger. A shorter, sharper portfolio beats a longer one with a few tired images in it.

Your step

Set a recurring calendar reminder every six months to review your portfolio and cut the weakest images.

Lesson 5 of 49 · 3 min read

Put together a camera kit you can rely on

Clients don't care whether you own the newest camera. They care that you show up and every photo gets delivered. Reliability matters far more than having the latest model, so build your kit around not failing.

A dependable starting kit usually includes:

  • One main camera body you know inside out
  • A backup body, or at least a reliable backup camera option for paid work
  • Two or three lenses covering the distances you shoot most, for example a 35mm and an 85mm for portraits
  • Enough memory cards and batteries for a full day without stopping to offload

Before buying another lens, ask what you can't do today. If the honest answer is "nothing a client would notice," keep the money.

Test everything before a paid shoot, not during it. The day before, charge every battery, format your cards in the camera and take a few frames with each lens. That's when you find the battery that won't hold a charge or the card that throws an error, while there's still time to swap it.

Once your kit is worth real money, insure it. A single theft or accident shouldn't be able to stop you working. Ask an insurance agent or a photographers' association what cover makes sense for your gear.

Keep the kit simple and learn it well. Fumbling with unfamiliar settings in front of a client costs you more than a missing lens ever will.

Your step

The day before your next shoot, charge every battery, format your cards and take test frames with each lens.

Lesson 6 of 49 · 1 min read

Bring a backup camera body to every paid shoot

Cameras fail without warning, and at a wedding or an event there's no second chance at the first kiss or the speeches. If your only body dies, the shoot is over, and so is the client's trust in you.

Bring a second body to every paid job. If you don't own one yet, rent one for big shoots or borrow from a photographer friend. Before the shoot, set both bodies up the same way: same picture settings, same file format, same custom buttons, and the clocks synced so the files sort in order later. Then switching takes seconds, instead of minutes of menu hunting while the moment passes.

Your step

Find a rental shop or photographer friend who could lend you a second body, and note it before your next paid shoot.

Lesson 7 of 49 · 1 min read

Insure your gear so one theft can't end your business

Replacing your cameras and lenses out of pocket could wipe out months of income and leave you unable to shoot the jobs you've already booked. A bag stolen from a car shouldn't be able to end your business.

This week, make a gear list: every item, its serial number and what it would cost to replace today. Keep it in the cloud with photos of each item and the receipts if you have them. Then talk to an insurance agent or a photographers' association about cover for your equipment, and ask about liability insurance too, since some venues want proof of it before you can shoot there. Check that the policy covers gear away from home and in your car, because that's where your kit spends its working life.

Your step

Make a list of every piece of gear with its serial number and replacement cost, and save it with photos in the cloud.
