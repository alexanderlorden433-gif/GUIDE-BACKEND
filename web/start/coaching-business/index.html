Lesson 1 of 49 · 3 min read

Choose a niche people can picture and refer

"Life coach" or "business consultant" is hard to market for a simple reason: nobody can picture who you're for. A friend who wants to recommend you can't think of the right person, and a stranger reading your profile can't tell whether you'd understand their problem. A clear niche fixes both.

A useful niche has two parts: who you help, and the specific result you help them reach. Compare "leadership coaching" with "helping first-time managers get through their first year leading a team." The second one makes people think of a name. That's what you want, because early on a lot of clients come through people who know you and know someone like that.

To find yours, start with what you already have:

  • Work or life experience you've been through yourself
  • Problems people already ask you for help with
  • A group you understand well and enjoy spending time with
  • A result you can describe in their words, not coaching words

Then write a draft in one line: "I help [who] get [result]." Read it to two or three people and ask who comes to mind. If they name someone, you're close. If they shrug, make the who or the result more specific.

Don't worry about picking the perfect niche forever. Plenty of coaches and consultants narrow further or shift focus once they see who they enjoy working with and where they get the best results. You're choosing a starting point you can test, not signing a lifetime contract. A narrow niche also doesn't stop you from taking a good client outside it. It just tells everyone else where to send people.

Your step

Write one line that says who you help and what result you help them get, then ask three people who it makes them think of.

Lesson 2 of 49 · 1 min read

Write for one kind of client, not everyone

When your website or profile tries to speak to everyone, nobody feels it was written for them. A busy parent, a startup founder and a recent retiree all read "I help people reach their potential" and keep scrolling, usually toward someone who names their exact situation.

Write one sentence that names who you help and the result you help them get, such as "I help new nurses build a work schedule they can keep up without burning out." Put it at the top of your website, your social profiles and your email signature. You can still accept a great client who falls outside it. The sentence is there so the right people recognize themselves in the first few seconds.

Your step

Write your one-sentence niche statement and put it at the top of your website and every social profile this week.

Lesson 3 of 49 · 3 min read

Know the line between coaching, therapy and consulting

Before you take a single client, get clear on what you're actually offering. Coaching, therapy and consulting are different jobs, and mixing them up causes problems for you and for the people you work with.

Coaching is forward-focused. You help a client set goals, take action and stay accountable, and you guide them to their own answers. Therapy deals with diagnosing and treating mental health conditions, and it's done by licensed professionals. Consulting means you bring the expertise: you give advice, or you do part of the work yourself.

Plenty of people blend coaching and consulting, and that's fine as long as the client knows which one they're buying. If they expect you to hand them a plan and you keep answering questions with questions, they'll feel shortchanged. If they want to find their own way and you keep prescribing, they'll feel steamrolled. Say it plainly on your website and in your agreement.

The line with therapy matters even more. If a client's needs clearly move into mental health treatment, referring them to a licensed professional is the ethical thing to do, and it protects you too. What coaches can and can't do varies by place, so check the rules where you live.

Prepare what you'll say before you need it, not in the middle of a session. Calm and short works best, for example: "This sounds really important, and it's outside what coaching is designed for. A licensed therapist could help you more with this part. Would you like help finding one?" Keep a short list of local or online options so you can follow through. You're not abandoning the client. You're making sure they get the right help.

Your step

Write the two or three sentences you'd use to refer a client to a therapist, and list a few places you could point them to.

Lesson 4 of 49 · 3 min read

Turn what you already do into a signature framework

When a prospect asks how coaching with you works, "we'll just talk it through" sounds vague, even if your sessions are excellent. A signature framework solves that. It's a named, step-by-step process you take clients through, and it makes your approach concrete enough to picture before they pay.

Don't invent one from scratch. The best frameworks come from what you already do with clients who get results. Look back at a few of them, or at how you'd help a friend with the same problem, and write down the steps in order. What happens first? What usually has to be true before the next stage works?

Then shape it:

  1. Group the steps into three to five stages
  2. Give each stage a short, plain name, such as Clarify, Plan, Build and Sustain
  3. Write one line on what the client gets out of each stage
  4. Note roughly how long each stage takes

Keep the names simple. A clever acronym is fine if it helps people remember, but clarity beats cleverness. Your client should be able to tell a friend, "We're in the planning stage now," and know what that means.

A framework also pays off later. Once your process is written down, it's easier to create workbooks and templates, hand parts of delivery to someone else, or turn the same method into a group program or course. It keeps your quality steady when you're busy, because you're not reinventing each engagement. Expect to refine it as you work with more clients. Version one just needs to be honest about what you actually do.

Your step

List the steps you take a client through from start to finish, then group them into three to five named stages.

Lesson 5 of 49 · 1 min read

Put your framework on one page for prospects

A prospect who can see the path from where they are to where they want to be finds it much easier to say yes. A one-page overview of your framework gives them that, and it's something they can forward to a partner or manager who wasn't on the call.

Map your process into three to five named stages with one line explaining each, plus how long the engagement runs and what's included. Keep it to a single page with plenty of white space. Send it right after discovery calls with a short note, such as "Here's the path we talked about. Happy to answer any questions."

Your step

Make a one-page overview with your three to five stages and one line each, ready to send after your next discovery call.

Lesson 6 of 49 · 3 min read

Choose how to price: hourly, packages or retainers

How you price shapes how clients show up, not just what they pay. Most coaches and consultants use one of three models, and each one fits a different kind of need.

Hourly pricing is the easiest to understand. The downside is that it can feel transactional. Clients start watching the clock and deciding session by session whether it's worth continuing, and they may hold back from reaching out between sessions, which is often exactly when they need support.

Packages bundle a set number of sessions over a defined period, sold as one commitment. Say you offer six sessions over three months for $900, paid upfront or in installments. Because both of you have committed to the whole process, clients tend to follow through, and you can plan the work around a real outcome instead of a single hour. For most new coaches, a package is the simplest place to start.

Retainers are ongoing monthly access, and they're more common in consulting than in one-on-one coaching. They suit a client whose need doesn't end, such as a founder who wants a thinking partner every month or a small business that needs regular marketing advice. For you, they bring steadier, more predictable revenue than one-off projects.

Whichever you choose, define exactly what the price includes: the number and length of sessions, support between them, and any materials. A clear package is easier to sell than a rate, because the client is buying a result and a plan instead of your time. You can always add an hourly option later for past clients who want a single check-in.

Your step

Sketch one package: how many sessions, over how long, what's included between sessions, and an example price.

Lesson 7 of 49 · 1 min read

Lower the scope, not just the price

When a prospect asks if you can do it for less, it's tempting to just drop the price. But giving the same work for less trains clients to haggle, and it quietly tells them your full price was inflated in the first place.

Instead, change what they get. Keep a lighter option ready, such as four sessions instead of six, or the same sessions without messaging support in between. Then you can say: "My rate is the same for everyone, but here's a smaller version that fits that budget." The prospect still gets a real choice, and your price stays honest.

Your step

Design a lighter version of your main package with fewer sessions or less support, so it's ready when someone asks.
