Lesson 1 of 49 · 3 min read

Pick the kind of writing you'll sell

"Freelance writer" covers a lot of different jobs, and clients hire for the job, not the title. Before you build samples or set prices, decide which kind of writing you're actually selling.

The biggest split is between copywriting and content writing. Copywriting is persuasive: it exists to get someone to do something, like buy, sign up or book a call. Think sales pages, ads, product descriptions and email sequences. Content writing is informational: it teaches, explains and builds trust over time. Think blog posts, articles, guides and newsletters. The skills overlap, but a great blog writer isn't automatically a great sales page writer, and the two are often priced differently.

A few other specialties are worth knowing about:

  • Technical writing: manuals, help docs and documentation where accuracy matters more than flair
  • UX writing: the words inside apps and websites, like buttons, error messages and sign-up screens
  • Ghostwriting: articles, posts or books published under the client's name instead of yours

Each has its own conventions and client expectations, so read real examples of any you're curious about before you offer it.

Then narrow down to one or two. "I write email sequences for online fitness coaches" is much easier to market than "I write anything." It tells the right client you're for them. It makes pricing simpler, because you're quoting similar jobs again and again. And every piece you write adds to one focused portfolio instead of five thin ones.

You can always widen later. Starting narrow simply makes your first clients easier to find and easier to convince.

Your step

Write one sentence that says what you write and for whom, like "I write email sequences for online fitness coaches."

Lesson 2 of 49 · 1 min read

Turn down work outside your focus

Early on, it's tempting to say yes to every writing job. But a topic you know nothing about can take hours of extra research, and the finished piece rarely shows your best work. Put it in your portfolio and it tends to attract more requests just like it, pulling you away from the niche you picked.

When a request doesn't fit, say so kindly. Thank the client, explain that it's outside what you focus on, and if you know a writer who covers that area, pass along their name. Something like: "Thanks for thinking of me. Crypto isn't my area, but a writer I know covers it really well." People remember that kind of honesty, and some will come back when they have a project that does fit.

Your step

Write a short, friendly reply for declining off-topic requests that includes a referral to another writer.

Lesson 3 of 49 · 3 min read

Build a portfolio before your first client

Clients want to see what you can do, and you don't need to have been paid yet to show them. A good portfolio piece proves you can write the kind of thing they need. Who paid for it matters much less.

Start with spec pieces. These are samples you write on your own, for a real or made-up brand, exactly as if you'd been hired. A few ideas:

  • A three-email welcome sequence for a local gym or an invented skincare brand
  • A rewrite of a weak landing page you found, shown next to the original
  • A set of product descriptions for a small online shop

Label them honestly as spec work. Clients won't mind. What they care about is whether the writing is good and fits their world.

Next, think about a blog or newsletter in the niche you want to write for. It does two jobs at once: every post is a fresh sample, and a steady publishing record shows you can deliver on schedule. Many clients value that reliability almost as much as the writing itself. You don't need a big audience. A dozen useful posts written over a few months says more than one brilliant post and then silence.

Finally, try guest posting. Plenty of blogs and publications in your niche accept contributions, and some don't pay. Early on, an unpaid guest post on a respected site can still be worth it, because it gives you a real byline and a published link you can share. Pick outlets your future clients actually read, and treat each pitch like paid work.

Put your best three or four pieces on a simple page, with a line about who each one was for, and you have a portfolio.

Your step

Choose one spec piece to write this week, like a three-email welcome sequence for a brand in your niche, and outline it today.

Lesson 4 of 49 · 1 min read

Keep a swipe file of copy you admire

A swipe file is a collection of writing you admire, saved so you can study it later. When a deadline is close and nothing is coming, a few strong examples give you a structure to start from. You're not copying their words. You're borrowing the shape: how the headline makes a promise, how the email opens, where the offer appears.

Set up folders by type: headlines, emails, sales pages and social posts. Each time a line grabs you, whether it's a subject line in your inbox or an ad in your feed, save it in the right folder with a one-line note on why it works. "Opens with a question the reader can't ignore" will be far more useful to you later than the example on its own.

Your step

Create four folders named headlines, emails, sales pages and social posts, and save three examples with a note on why each works.

Lesson 5 of 49 · 3 min read

Write samples for the clients you want

A prospective client looking at your portfolio is asking one question: has this person written something like what I need? A beautifully written blog post about coffee doesn't answer that for a software company that needs a landing page. A solid landing page for a made-up software tool does.

That's why samples written for your target niche usually beat general ones, even when the general ones are more polished. They show you already understand the reader, the vocabulary and the goal. If you want to write for software companies, your samples should be landing pages, onboarding emails and feature announcements. If you want to write for dentists and physiotherapists, write service pages and patient emails.

Aim for two or three strong spec pieces in that niche rather than ten pieces across ten topics. A focused portfolio is quicker to build and easier for a client to judge. For each piece, add a short note on the brief you set yourself: who it's for, what it needs to achieve, and why you made the choices you did. That note shows your thinking, which is often what clients are really buying.

Then treat your portfolio as something that keeps changing. When you land paid work in a category, replace the matching spec piece with the real one as soon as the client is happy for you to share it. Over time, the spec samples should disappear and real client work should take their place.

Every few months, look through it and remove anything that no longer represents your best work, or the work you want more of.

Your step

List three pieces a client in your niche would pay for, then pick the one you'll write first as a spec sample.

Lesson 6 of 49 · 1 min read

Show your impact with before-and-after edits

A finished piece doesn't show what you changed. A prospect reading your polished sales page has no idea whether you wrote it from scratch or rescued a messy draft. Put the original next to your version and your value becomes obvious in seconds.

Keep a folder of a few strong before-and-after pairs. For client work, ask permission first. You can also use spec rewrites, like a weak landing page you improved for practice. Under each pair, add one line on what changed or what happened next, for example "cut the page by a third and moved the offer to the top." Share these on sales calls, or attach one to a pitch when a client mentions their current copy isn't working.

Your step

Pick one piece of weak copy, rewrite it, and save the original and your version side by side with one line on what changed.
