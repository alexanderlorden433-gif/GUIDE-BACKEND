Is it right for you?

What it is

A one-person service where you clean homes and small offices, and pressure wash driveways, patios, siding and decks. Customers pay for a finished result and saved time.

  • Home and small-office cleaning
  • Driveway, patio, siding and deck washing
  • Paid per job or on a repeat schedule

Who it's for

People who are reliable, don't mind physical work and can talk to neighbors. You don't need a degree, but you do need to be trustworthy and careful.

  • Dependable and on time, every time
  • Fine with physical, hands-on work
  • Comfortable asking for work locally

A normal week

A mix of jobs, estimates and admin: some cleans or washes, a few quotes, photos, messages and bookkeeping. Weather can move outdoor jobs around.

  • Jobs on set days, estimates in between
  • Weather can shift outdoor washing days
  • An hour or two for messages and books

What it costs to start

Cleaning needs a basic kit. A pressure washer costs more, but you can rent first. Add insurance and any local registration. Costs vary a lot, so get real quotes.

  • Cleaning supplies and a vacuum
  • Washer and nozzles, or rent at first
  • Insurance, registration and a phone plan

Tools you'll use

Cloths, brushes and cleaners for homes. A pressure washer, hoses, nozzles and a surface cleaner for outdoors. Safety gear, a phone and a calendar.

  • Microfiber cloths, mop and vacuum
  • Pressure washer and surface cleaner
  • Google Business Profile and a calendar

The hard parts

The work is physical and weather can cancel jobs. The first months are often slow, some customers are picky, and a wrong move can damage property.

  • Physical work and sore muscles
  • A slow start while people get to know you
  • Damage risk, so test first and get insured

Lesson 1 of 24 · 3 min read

What cleaning and pressure washing jobs really look like

This business has two sides that look alike from the outside but work very differently. Cleaning means going into homes and small offices and leaving them tidy: kitchens, bathrooms, floors, dusting, trash. Pressure washing means going outside with a machine and blasting dirt, mildew and grime off driveways, patios, siding and decks.

Customers hire you for the same reasons on both sides. They don't have the time, they don't enjoy it, or the job needs gear they don't own. What they're really paying for is a finished result and a free afternoon.

Cleaning is mostly indoor, repeat work. The same customer may book you every two weeks. It needs few tools, but it needs a lot of trust, because you're inside people's homes, around their things. Pressure washing is more seasonal and more visible. It needs a machine and more care, since the wrong pressure can damage a surface. It also gives you dramatic before and after photos, which help you find customers.

A normal job goes like this. The customer contacts you. You agree what's included and the price. You show up on time, do the work in the same order every time, check the result with the customer, and get paid. Most of the skill is in the agreeing and the checking, not just the scrubbing.

Be honest with yourself about the hard parts. The work is physical. Weather can cancel outdoor jobs. Some customers are picky, and the first months are often slow while people get to know you.

The good news is that it's a simple business to start. You need a few tools, a clear offer, good habits and the nerve to ask the people around you for work. This module walks you through setting that up.

Your step

Walk around your block and list five places that need cleaning or washing, and which service each one would need.

Lesson 2 of 24 · 1 min read

Pick one side to start with

You can offer both sides someday. On day one, pick one. Two sets of tools, two kinds of customers and two ways to get things wrong is a lot for a beginner.

Ask yourself four questions. What can I afford? Cleaning needs a basket of supplies. Pressure washing needs a machine and attachments, though you can rent. Where do I want to work? Cleaning happens indoors in any weather. Washing depends on the weather and on local water rules. What am I comfortable doing? Some people like being inside a home, others prefer being outdoors with a machine. And who do I already know? Friends and neighbors with a dirty driveway or a busy week are your first customers.

Choose one, and give it a few months before you add the other.

Your step

Write one sentence: "I'll start with [cleaning or pressure washing] because [your reason]." Keep it where you'll see it.

Set up your business simply and legally

Before your first paid job, set up the basics. None of it is hard, but the rules depend on where you live, so check them locally instead of trusting a general guide, including this one.

Start with a name. Your own name works, or pick a simple business name. Search your area to make sure nobody nearby already uses it.

Next, ask your city, county or state what a business like yours needs. Some places ask you to register the business or get a local business license. Some license pressure washing or home-service work, and others don't. The websites for your city, county and state usually list this, and a call to the local business office works too. Ask what a one-person cleaning and pressure washing business needs.

Then talk to an insurance agent. You'll be working in other people's homes, with strong cleaners and a machine that can damage property. Ask about general liability insurance for cleaning or pressure washing, what it covers if you damage something, whether your personal car or home policy still applies when you use it for work, and what it costs. Customers will sometimes ask if you're insured, so have your answer ready. If you hire helpers later, ask what extra cover or rules apply.

Open a separate bank account for the business, even a basic one. It keeps income and costs clear, which makes taxes much easier. A qualified accountant or your local tax office can tell you what you owe and when.

Write each answer down as you find it. You'll use that list again when you set your prices.

Your step

Find your city or county's business license page and write down what it says, plus one question for an insurance agent.

Lesson 4 of 24 · 1 min read

Check water runoff and chemical rules

Wash water doesn't just disappear. It carries dirt, oil, soap and chemicals, and it often runs into a storm drain, a stream or a neighbor's garden. Many places have rules about this, and they differ a lot by country, state and city.

Ask your local environmental or water office what applies to you. Can wash water go into a storm drain? Do I need to collect or filter it for some jobs? Are some cleaning products limited?

While you wait for answers, lower the risk. Use the mildest product that does the job. Wet and rinse plants and lawns so soap and chemicals stay off them. Never pour leftover chemicals down a storm drain, and follow the label for disposal. If you're not sure, ask before you wash, not after.

Your step

Search your city or county name plus "wash water storm drain rules", write down what you find, or call the local environmental office.

Lesson 5 of 24 · 3 min read

Write a first offer with three services or fewer

A first offer should fit on one phone screen. When you offer everything, people don't know what to ask for, and you can't price or do any of it well. When you offer three things clearly, people remember them.

Here are a few examples of narrow offers. A standard home clean, a deep clean of kitchen and bathrooms, and a move-out clean. Or a driveway and walkway wash, a patio wash, and a siding wash. Or small-office cleaning after hours. Pick the ones that match your gear, your time and what your neighbors seem to want.

For each service, write down what's included. A standard clean might mean kitchen, bathrooms, floors, dusting and trash, and might not include inside the oven, inside the fridge or windows. Anything not on the list is an add-on with its own price. This one habit prevents most arguments later.

Then set your limits:

  1. Where you'll travel, for example a 20-minute drive from home
  2. Which days and hours you'll work
  3. How big a job you'll take at first

Finally, decide what you'll refuse. A good beginner list includes mold problems, biohazards, hoarding, roofs, anything that needs a ladder, and surfaces you don't know how to treat safely. Saying "that's not something I do, but I can help with this" is a professional answer, not a failure.

You can widen the offer later, once your first customers are happy. A narrow offer gets you started.

Your step

Write your offer: up to three services, the area you'll cover, your working days, and three jobs you'll say no to.

Lesson 6 of 24 · 1 min read

Do a practice job and time it

Don't make a paying customer your first test. Do a full practice job at your own home, a relative's place or a friend's driveway, with their permission.

Treat it like the real thing. Write the job down first: which rooms or surfaces, and what's included. Pack your supplies. Set a timer and note the time for each part, such as bathroom, kitchen and floors, or driveway and walkway. Take before and after photos. Notice what slowed you down and what you forgot to bring.

Afterward, ask the owner to look over the result and say what they'd want done differently.

If a three-bedroom home takes you three hours, that's a real number to use when you quote. Expect to be slower than an experienced cleaner at first, and plan for it.

Your step

Clean your own kitchen and bathroom, or wash your own driveway or patio, with a timer, and note how long each part took.
