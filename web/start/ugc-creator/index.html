Lesson 1 of 48 · 3 min read

What UGC is and how it differs from influencing

UGC stands for user-generated content. As a UGC creator, you make videos and photos that look and feel like they came from a real customer, and a brand uses them in its own marketing: its ads, its social pages, its product pages and emails. The brand is paying for the content itself, not for the people who follow you.

That's the big difference from influencer marketing. An influencer is paid partly for access to their audience, so follower count drives the price. A UGC creator is paid for the video. It runs on the brand's account or in their ads, and it may never appear on yours. That's why you can start with zero followers. Your product is the content, not your platform.

Most UGC falls into a handful of formats you already see in your feed:

  • A review, where you share an honest take after using the product
  • An unboxing, where you open it and react on camera
  • A demo or tutorial, where you show how it works
  • A problem and solution story, where the annoyance comes first and the product fixes it
  • A testimonial, where you explain why you'd recommend it

Brands want this style because it looks like a recommendation from a real person, not a commercial. People tend to scroll straight past anything that feels like a polished ad, but they'll often stop for someone in their kitchen holding a product and talking to the camera. Your job is to make content that feels that natural while still hitting every point the brand needs.

Keep that in mind for the rest of this course. Brands aren't hiring you for studio lighting or a famous face. They're hiring you to make their product feel real to the person watching.

Your step

Save five UGC-style ads from your own feed this week and label each one as a review, unboxing, demo, problem and solution, or testimonial.

Lesson 2 of 48 · 1 min read

Keep a swipe file of UGC ads that work

A swipe file is a folder of ads you've saved because they made you stop scrolling. It's not for copying. It's for studying how good UGC opens, how fast it moves and how it ends, because those patterns are what brands already know work for them.

Whenever an ad grabs you, save it or screen-record it into one folder. Next to each one, jot down three things: the hook, meaning the first line or image, the structure, like problem then product then result, and the call to action at the end. After twenty or so, you'll start to see the same few shapes over and over. Borrow the shape, then fill it with your own ideas, your own words and your own voice.

Your step

Create a swipe file folder today and save three UGC ads, noting the hook, structure and call to action for each.

Lesson 3 of 48 · 3 min read

Build a portfolio before your first paid deal

Brands want to see your work before they hire you, but you can't show work until someone hires you. Spec content breaks that loop. A spec video is one you make on your own, unpaid, for a product you choose, purely to show future clients what you can do. Most UGC creators build their first portfolio exactly this way.

Pick three to five products you already own and genuinely like: a skincare product, a kitchen gadget, a water bottle, your dog's favorite treats. Real use shows on camera, and you'll have honest things to say. If you can, choose products from the kind of brands you'd like to work with, so your samples match the niche you're aiming for.

Then make the kind of video a brand would actually run, not a vague clip of you saying you love something. Each one needs a real product moment:

  • A demo showing the product doing its job
  • A problem and solution story, like frizzy hair before and smooth hair after
  • An unboxing with a genuine first reaction
  • A short review with one specific reason you'd recommend it

Aim for variety, so a brand can picture you making several kinds of content for them. Keep each video short, like the ads you see in your feed, and give every one a clear opening line and a clear ending.

Put the finished videos in one simple place, such as a portfolio page, a link-in-bio page or a short reel. Group them by content type, so someone skimming for thirty seconds sees your demos together and your unboxings together. A busy brand marketer should understand your style before they've watched a single video all the way through.

Your step

Choose three products you own and write one spec video idea for each: a demo, a problem and solution, and an unboxing.

Lesson 4 of 48 · 1 min read

Hook viewers in the first two seconds

In a scrolling feed, people decide almost instantly whether to keep watching. If your video opens with "Hi guys, so today..." they're gone before the product ever appears. Brands judge your videos by how long people watch, so a slow start costs you on every video.

Open with something that earns the next few seconds: a bold claim ("This is the only sunscreen that doesn't sting my eyes"), a question ("Why does nobody talk about this?"), or a visual, like the product's result already on screen. Start mid-action, with no greeting and no introduction.

Before you film the rest of any script, write three possible opening lines and pick the strongest. The hook is the line most worth your time.

Your step

Take one of your spec video ideas and write three different opening lines for it before you film anything.

Lesson 5 of 48 · 1 min read

Test different hooks on the same video

The hook decides whether anyone watches the rest of your video, and you can't always tell from your own desk which one works best. Testing takes the guesswork out. It also gives you something concrete to say in a pitch: you didn't just pick a hook, you checked it.

Film the same video three times with three different opening lines, keeping everything after the hook the same. Show each version to a few friends or followers and pay attention to one thing: which opening keeps them watching longest. Write down which style won, whether it was a question, a bold claim or a visual, and open your next spec videos that way.

Your step

Pick one spec video, film it with three different opening lines, and show all three versions to a few friends.

Lesson 6 of 48 · 1 min read

End every video with a clear call to action

Brands pay for results, and a viewer who liked your video but doesn't know what to do next is a lost sale. A call to action is the closing line that tells them: tap the link, grab the starter kit, try it for yourself. Unless the brief says otherwise, every video should have one.

Keep it to one simple instruction tied to the brand's goal. If they want sales, point people to the shop. If they're growing an email list, mention the discount for signing up. Say it out loud and show it as on-screen text, so people watching with the sound off still get it.

Make it sound like you, not a script. "Honestly, just try it, the link's right there" feels far more natural than a stiff sales line.

Your step

Add a one-line call to action to each of your spec video scripts, and plan the on-screen text that goes with it.

Lesson 7 of 48 · 1 min read

Shoot vertical unless the brief says otherwise

Most brands run creator content in short-form vertical feeds, where a horizontal clip gets cropped awkwardly or simply rejected. Unless a brief asks for something else, vertical 9:16 is your default.

Set your phone or camera to portrait before every shoot. Leave some breathing room at the top and bottom of the frame, because that's where the app's buttons, the captions and the account name will sit. Keep your face and the product near the center, where nothing will cover them.

If a brief asks for horizontal or square, follow the brief. Some brands also use footage on their website or in other placements, and they'll tell you when they do.

Your step

Set your camera to vertical and film a ten-second test clip, checking that your face and product sit clear of the top and bottom.

Lesson 8 of 48 · 1 min read

Film in natural light whenever you can

Soft daylight from a window makes skin, food and products look the way they do in real life. That matters in UGC, because the moment a video looks like a studio ad, viewers start to treat it like one. A ring light can work in a pinch, but it often gives a flat look, and the circle reflected in your eyes is a giveaway.

Face a window during the brightest part of your day, with the light falling on you and the product, not coming from behind you. Avoid harsh direct sun, which throws hard shadows. If the sun is streaming in, a sheer curtain softens it nicely.

Spend one day testing your home: film a quick clip in each room at a few different times and note which spot gives you the best light. That's your filming spot from now on.

Your step

Film a five-second test clip by each window in your home at two different times of day, and note the best spot.

Lesson 9 of 48 · 1 min read

Get a lavalier mic for clearer audio

People will put up with slightly shaky footage, but muddy or echoey sound makes them leave, and brands reject clips where the voiceover is hard to hear. Your phone's built-in mic picks up the room as much as your voice. A lavalier, the small mic that clips to your clothes, sits close to your mouth and fixes that for relatively little money.

Clip it to your collar about a hand's width below your chin and run the cable under your shirt so it stays out of shot. Before every session, record a short test and listen back with headphones, not your phone speaker. You'll catch rustling, hum or a loose connection while it's still easy to fix.

Your step

Order a basic lavalier mic that works with your phone, then record a test clip and play it back through headphones.
