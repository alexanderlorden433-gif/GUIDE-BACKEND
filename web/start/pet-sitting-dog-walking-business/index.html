Is it right for you?

What it is

You walk dogs, drop in to feed and care for pets, and stay at the owner's home when they are away. It is not a kennel or daycare.

  • Dog walks of about 20 to 60 minutes
  • Drop-in visits for food, water, play and litter
  • Overnight or all-day sitting in the owner's home

Who it's for

People who are reliable, calm around animals and happy to work outdoors in any weather, in a place where many households have pets.

  • Dependable and on time, even in the rain
  • Calm with animals of every mood
  • Comfortable being trusted with someone's home

A normal week

Short, regular blocks of walks and visits, with messages, photo updates and meet-and-greets fitted in around them.

  • Busy around mornings, lunchtime and evenings
  • A short photo update after each visit
  • Holidays and school breaks are often the busiest

What it costs to start

Mainly insurance, a few good leashes, bags and a first aid kit, plus any business registration your area requires. Costs vary by place.

  • Business insurance and any bonding
  • Leashes, poop bags, towels and a first aid kit
  • Business registration, if your area requires it

Tools you'll use

A phone, a calendar, a messaging app and a way to take payments, plus a free Google Business Profile so neighbors can find you.

  • Google Calendar, or scheduling software like Jobber
  • Square, Stripe or PayPal to get paid
  • Google Business Profile and a care sheet for each pet

The hard parts

Bad weather, early starts, scared or difficult pets, last-minute cancellations, holiday crunches and the weight of being responsible for a pet.

  • Full schedules on days of rain, heat or ice
  • Pets who are scared, sick or hard to handle
  • A slow start while you build trust and reviews

Lesson 1 of 24 · 3 min read

What dog walking, drop-ins and pet sitting really involve

Pet care work means looking after other people's animals while they are at work or away. Most beginners start with three services. A dog walk is a set stretch of time, often 20 to 60 minutes, when you take one or more dogs out. A drop-in visit is a short stop at the home to feed the pet, give fresh water, clean a litter box, play and let a dog out. In-home pet sitting means you stay at the owner's home overnight, or for a full day, so the pet keeps its normal routine. This chapter does not cover running a kennel or daycare at your own place. That is a different business with different rules.

A normal day is more than the fun part. You will pick up poop in the rain. You will move between homes on a tight schedule. You will deal with dogs that pull, cats that hide and owners who text you at 9 pm. Some weeks are busy and some are quiet, and holidays are often the hardest time, because everyone travels at once.

What owners are really buying is peace of mind. They hand you a key, an animal they love and access to their home. So the job rests on trust, good habits and clear communication more than on a love of animals alone. Loving animals helps. Being reliable, calm and careful matters more.

You do not need special training to begin, but you do need to learn safe handling, how to keep a home secure and what to do in an emergency. Those come later in this chapter. For now, ask yourself one honest question: which of these jobs would you still be happy to do three times a week in bad weather? That is the work.

Your step

Write the three services on a page and mark the one that best fits your schedule and your comfort with animals.

Lesson 2 of 24 · 1 min read

Start with one or two services in a small area

It is tempting to say yes to everything, including long drives across town. Resist that at the start. Pick one or two services, such as weekday dog walks plus drop-in visits, and keep to an area you can cross in about ten minutes. Short travel time means more of your day is paid work and less of it is spent in traffic. It also means you can reach a pet quickly if something goes wrong.

A short menu helps you get good. You learn the local dogs, the best routes and the gates that stick. Customers also remember you more easily. "She walks dogs on our street" travels further by word of mouth than a long list of services. You can add overnight sitting or other animals later, once your safety habits are solid.

Your step

Draw a circle on a map around your home that you can cross in ten minutes, and pick the one or two services you will offer inside it.

Lesson 3 of 24 · 3 min read

Check your local rules and register simply

Rules for pet care are not the same everywhere. Some places ask nothing of a dog walker beyond an ordinary business registration. Others have extra rules for walkers, for sitters, or for anyone who looks after animals for pay. Boarding and daycare, where animals come to your own place, are far more likely to need a license, inspections or special zoning. That is one reason to begin with walks and visits at the owner's home. This course cannot tell you what your area requires, so you need to check your local rules yourself.

Here is a simple way to do that:

  1. Search your city or county website for "pet sitter", "dog walker" and "business license".
  2. Look up how to register a small business in your state or country, and whether a business name has to be filed if it is not your own name.
  3. Ask whether there are limits on how many dogs one person may walk at once, or permits for using parks and trails commercially.
  4. If you rent or live in a condo, read your lease and building rules before running any part of the business from home.
  5. Call and ask. Most local offices will answer a short question. Write down who you spoke to and when.

Many people start as a sole proprietor, which is the simplest way to work alone, but the right setup depends on where you live and your own situation. A local small business advisor, an accountant or a lawyer can tell you which fits. In the US, a Small Business Development Center is a good place to start. Checking now costs very little compared with a fine, or a business shut down later.

Your step

Search your city or county website for pet sitter and dog walker rules, and write down the name and number of the office to call to confirm.

Lesson 4 of 24 · 1 min read

Ask an insurance agent about pet care cover

Before you take a pet into your care, talk to an insurance agent who knows small businesses. Ask about three things. First, general liability, which can help if someone is hurt or property is damaged because of your work. Second, whether the policy covers animals in your care, custody or control. Many general policies leave out injury or loss to the animal itself, so this cover often has to be added on purpose. Third, bonding, a separate protection that can reimburse a client if something goes missing from their home. Many owners like to see it.

Ask what is excluded, what the deductible is, and whether walks, car trips and overnight stays are all covered. Rules and prices differ by insurer and by place, so collect at least two quotes.

Your step

Call one independent insurance agent, ask for a quote for dog walking and pet sitting cover, and write down what it leaves out.

Lesson 5 of 24 · 3 min read

Run a meet-and-greet that earns the job

Before you take a pet on, ask for a meet-and-greet at the owner's home. Plan on 20 to 30 minutes. Most owners will not hand over a key until they have met you, and you should not accept one until you have met the pet.

Greet the owner first, not the dog. Let the pet come to you and do not rush to pet it. Many dogs are wary of strangers, and cats may hide. A pet who cannot settle around you now may not be a good fit for a visit when the owner is away.

Then listen and look. Ask about:

  1. Feeding times, amounts, treats and anything the pet must never eat
  2. Medicines, health problems, and the name and number of the vet
  3. Fears, such as thunder, other dogs, visitors or the vacuum cleaner
  4. Behavior on the leash, at the door and around strangers
  5. Where the leash, bags, food and cleaning supplies are kept
  6. How the owner wants updates, and how often

Walk through the parts of the home you will use and note anything risky, such as a gate that does not latch, a door that opens to the street, or the place the cat hides. Ask the owner before taking any photos.

End by saying what happens next. Offer to send your service agreement and a care sheet to fill in. Also remember that you can say no. A dog that has bitten someone, or a home where you feel uneasy, is a fair reason to pass. You are choosing clients too.

Your step

Write ten questions to ask at a meet-and-greet, including one about anything that scares or upsets the pet.

Lesson 6 of 24 · 1 min read

Fill in a care sheet for every pet

A care sheet is one page per pet, filled in by the owner and checked by you at the meet-and-greet. It saves you from relying on memory.

Include the pet's name, age and breed, a recent photo, and microchip or tag details. Add feeding times and amounts, medicines and how they are given, and any health problems. List the vet's name and number, plus an emergency clinic nearby. Note habits and fears, like pulling on the leash or hiding when the doorbell rings. Write down where supplies are kept, door and alarm instructions, and a backup contact.

Keep the sheets on your phone, protected by a passcode. Ask owners to update the sheet whenever something changes, and collect only the personal details you need.

Your step

Make a one-page care sheet with headings for food, medicine, habits and contacts, and use it as your master copy for owners to fill in.
