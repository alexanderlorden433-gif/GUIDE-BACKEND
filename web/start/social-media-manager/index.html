Module 1: Start here: shape your service

Lesson 1 of 49 · 3 min read

Decide exactly what your service includes

Ask five business owners what "social media management" means and you'll get five answers. To one it's scheduling posts they already made. To another it's photos, videos, captions, replies to every message and a few ads on top. If you don't define the term for your business, each client will define it for you, usually in the biggest way possible.

Here's what the phrase can cover:

  • Scheduling and posting content
  • Creating content: graphics, photos, short videos and captions
  • Community management: replying to comments and messages
  • Paid ads and influencer outreach
  • Monthly reporting

Each of these takes different skills and very different amounts of time. Posting five finished graphics might take an hour. Filming and editing three short videos can take a whole day. Decide now which services make up your core offer, which you'll sell as extras, and which you'll refer to someone else or decline. That choice shapes your prices, the kind of client you go after, and how many accounts you can realistically handle.

A sensible starting point is content creation, scheduling, replying to comments and a short monthly report, on one or two platforms. Ads and influencer work can wait until you've learned them properly.

Then be honest about results. Social media management doesn't promise viral posts or overnight growth, and saying so at the start protects the relationship later. A client who expects steady, gradual progress stays calm in a slow month. A client who was sold on explosive growth starts looking for someone else.

Your step

Write one paragraph describing your core service: what's included, what costs extra, and what you don't offer at all.

Lesson 2 of 49 · 1 min read

Promise the work, not follower numbers

When a prospect asks how many followers you'll get them, it's tempting to name a number. Don't. Growth depends on things you don't control: platform algorithms, the client's budget, their product and their prices. Promise 1,000 new followers, deliver 400, and you're set up for a refund argument even if your work was excellent.

Promise what you do control instead. In your proposal, list specific deliverables: posts per week, how quickly you'll reply to comments, and a report on a set date each month. When a client asks about growth, explain what you'll test in the first few months and how you'll show them progress in your reports.

Your step

Go through your proposal or service description and turn every promise into a deliverable you control, like posts per week or reply times.

Lesson 3 of 49 · 3 min read

Choose the right platforms, not all of them

Offering to run every platform for a new client looks generous, and it pads your invoice. It often does little for their business. For most small businesses, two platforms done well beat five done halfway, and they give you clearer results to show in your reports.

Start with one question: where do this client's customers already spend time? A plumber's customers search Google and ask neighbors in local Facebook groups, so a well-kept Google Business Profile and an active Facebook page may matter far more than TikTok. A boutique selling to younger shoppers might live on Instagram and TikTok. A business consultant probably belongs on LinkedIn. Ignore what's trendy this month and follow the customers.

To find out, ask the client:

  • Where do new customers say they found you?
  • Which accounts or platforms do your best customers follow?
  • Where do your competitors get real comments, not just likes?
  • How much time can you give us for photos, videos and approvals?

That last question matters more than it looks. Short-form video platforms need a steady supply of footage. If the client can't provide it, or pay you to create it, that platform will stall within weeks.

Then propose a focused plan: one or two main platforms, done properly, with the option to add more once those are running smoothly. If the budget is tight, say so plainly. Spreading a small budget across many platforms usually produces thin results everywhere and leaves you with nothing convincing to point to at renewal time.

Your step

Pick one local business you'd like as a client and write down the two platforms you'd recommend for them, with one sentence on why.

Lesson 4 of 49 · 1 min read

Decide which clients you'll turn down

Sooner or later a business will offer you good money to promote something you don't believe in. Maybe it's a product you think is misleading, or an industry you'd rather not be linked with. Saying yes is tempting, especially early on. But promoting something you don't believe in wears down your motivation, and your name ends up on work that future clients will judge you by.

Decide your limits before the offer arrives, while you can think clearly. Write down three or four kinds of businesses you won't work with. Add a question about the client's products to your intake form, so you spot a mismatch early and decline before you spend an evening on a proposal. A short reply is enough: "Thanks for thinking of me. This isn't the right fit, but I wish you the best with it."

Your step

Write down three or four kinds of businesses you won't work with, and add a screening question about them to your intake form.

Lesson 5 of 49 · 1 min read

Separate your business and personal accounts

Running client work through your personal profiles and your personal bank account feels simpler in week one. It gets messy fast. Client payments mix with grocery runs, which makes tax time harder. Your personal posts sit right next to your professional image. And if your personal account gets locked for any reason, you can lose access to every client page connected to it.

Fix it now, while it's easy. Open a separate bank account just for client payments and business costs. Set up business profiles for your own marketing on the platforms you work on. Then use only those business profiles to access client pages, through each platform's business tools. Ask an accountant which records you should keep for your situation.

Your step

Open a separate bank account for client payments this week, and create a business profile on the main platform you'll work on.

Lesson 6 of 49 · 3 min read

Build a portfolio before your first client

Every prospect wants to know one thing: can you get results for a business like mine? You don't need an agency history to answer that. You need proof, and you can start making it this week.

Start with an account you control. Use your own business profile, or a page for a friend's project or a local club, and run it the way you'd run a client's account: a plan, a posting schedule, replies to comments. Write down the starting numbers before you begin, such as followers, average engagement and profile visits. After six to eight weeks you'll have a real before-and-after, plus a story about what you tried and why.

Next, offer one business a pilot. Keep it limited, say one platform for two months at a reduced rate, and agree at the start that in return you'll get a detailed case study and a testimonial. Put that trade in writing, so asking for it later doesn't feel awkward. It's one of the most common ways new agencies earn their first proof.

When you build the portfolio, lead with results and strategy. Pretty grid screenshots don't tell a business owner much. A strong case study fits on one page:

  • The starting point, with real numbers
  • What you changed and why
  • What happened: engagement rate, follower growth or leads
  • A short quote from the client, if you have one

Two or three of these will persuade a prospect far more than a gallery of nice-looking posts.

Your step

Choose one account you can run for the next two months, and write down its followers, engagement and profile visits today.
