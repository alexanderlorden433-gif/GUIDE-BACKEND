Lesson 1 of 49 · 3 min read

Pick the kind of video work you'll lead with

Weddings, corporate work, music videos and real estate tours all use a camera, but they're different businesses. A wedding couple is buying emotion and reliability on one day that can't be repeated. A corporate client is buying polish, fast turnaround and someone who shows up looking professional, often again and again. A musician wants a look that stands out. A real estate agent wants bright, clean walkthroughs delivered quickly.

When one portfolio and one pitch try to speak to all of them, each buyer sees a little of what they want and a lot of what they don't. The engaged couple scrolls past a product video. The marketing manager sees a first dance and wonders whether you understand their brand. You come across as less experienced than you are.

Picking a lane doesn't mean saying no to everything else forever. It means your reel, your prices and the words on your website speak clearly to one kind of buyer. That clarity is what makes someone send an inquiry instead of moving on to the next videographer.

If you're not sure which lane fits, look back at what you've already shot, including unpaid projects for friends:

  • Which projects did you enjoy most?
  • Which ones got the strongest reactions from the people in them?
  • Which kind of client do you want to spend your weekdays or weekends with?

Those answers are usually a better guide than whatever looks most profitable on paper. You'll do better work, and stick with it longer, in a lane you enjoy. Choose one to lead with for the next six months and treat everything else as occasional work.

Your step

List your three favorite past projects, note what kind of client each was for, and pick one lane to lead with for six months.

Lesson 2 of 49 · 3 min read

Build a small kit you know inside out

It's tempting to believe the next camera body will make your work look professional. In practice, steady, reliable results come from knowing a smaller kit so well that you can change settings without looking, fix a problem calmly while the client watches, and pack the bag half asleep.

A practical starting kit usually looks like this:

  • One reliable camera body you know thoroughly
  • Two or three versatile lenses that cover wide, medium and close shots
  • An external microphone, because bad audio is one of the hardest things to fix in the edit
  • A basic lighting setup you can put up quickly
  • Enough batteries and memory cards to get through a full shoot day without stopping

Before you add variety, add redundancy. A third lens is nice to have. A spare battery and a spare card are essential, because one dead battery or failed card can end a shoot, while a limited lens choice almost never does. The same thinking applies to cables, chargers and adapters.

Then look after what you own. Clean lenses gently, charge everything the night before, and keep a written packing list in the bag so nothing gets left behind. Do a quick test recording before every job, sound included, so you find problems at home instead of on location.

When you do upgrade, buy to solve a specific problem you keep running into, like needing better low-light performance for evening receptions. A new model coming out isn't a reason on its own.

Your step

Write down every item in your kit, circle anything that's a single point of failure, and add a spare for it before your next shoot.

Lesson 3 of 49 · 1 min read

Pack a backup battery and spare cards

A dead battery or a corrupted card in the middle of a shoot means footage you can't get back, and a client who probably won't book you again. It's one of the most avoidable ways to ruin a job.

Pack a charged spare for every camera and two empty, formatted cards in a labeled pouch. Do the same for your audio recorder and any lights that run on batteries. Swap cards at every break, so if one fails you lose a few minutes of footage instead of the whole day.

Your step

Put a charged spare battery and two formatted cards in a labeled pouch in your camera bag tonight.

Lesson 4 of 49 · 1 min read

Format your memory cards the night before

Formatting a card on set takes time you don't have, and when you're rushing it's easy to format the wrong one, the card that's already full of footage. Doing it the night before removes both problems.

Format each empty card in the camera you'll actually use on the shoot. Then use a simple system so you always know which cards are safe to record on: store empty cards label side up, and flip each one label side down the moment it's full. Never format a full card on location, even if you think it's been copied. That waits until the footage is safely backed up at home.

Your step

Tonight, format your empty cards in the camera you'll use next and store them label side up in your card case.

Lesson 5 of 49 · 1 min read

Make three-point lighting your default setup

Three-point lighting is the setup most interviews and talking-head videos start from: a key light as the main source, a fill light on the other side to soften shadows, and a back light behind the subject to separate them from the background. It isn't the only way to light, but it's a dependable starting point.

Having a default matters because improvising in front of a client looks like guessing, and often it is. A setup you trust gives every subject flattering, consistent light, so your attention can go to framing and to putting people at ease.

Practice at home with a patient friend, or a lamp and a chair, until you can set all three lights in about ten minutes. On set, start from that setup, then adjust for the room, the window light and the look the client wants.

Your step

Set up a key, fill and back light at home and time yourself. Repeat until it takes about ten minutes.

Lesson 6 of 49 · 1 min read

Get gear and liability insurance early

Two kinds of insurance matter for most videographers. Gear cover pays to repair or replace equipment that's dropped, stolen or damaged. General liability cover helps if your work causes harm to someone else or their property, like a light stand that tips into a venue's wall or a guest who trips over your cable.

Either accident can cost more than you'd earn from months of shoots, which is why it's worth sorting before you get busy. Some venues also ask for proof of liability cover before they let you film.

Get quotes from two or three providers this week and compare what's actually covered, including gear left in your car and shoots away from home. Policies and requirements vary by country and region, so ask a local insurance broker if anything is unclear.

Your step

Request quotes for gear cover and general liability from two or three providers, and ask your usual venues if they need proof of cover.

Lesson 7 of 49 · 1 min read

Open a business bank account before your first gig

Open a separate account for your video work before the first client pays you, not six months in. When gig income and personal spending share one account, tax time turns into an evening of guessing which purchases were gear and which were groceries.

A separate account also answers a question that's surprisingly hard to answer otherwise: is this business actually making money? When every client payment comes in and every gear purchase, software subscription and travel cost goes out of one account, you can see it at a glance.

Open a business checking account this week and run every payment and expense through it. Ask an accountant which records you need to keep and how to set yourself up for tax where you live.

Your step

Open a business checking account this week and run your next client payment and gear purchase through it.
