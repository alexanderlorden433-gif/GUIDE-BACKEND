Is it right for you?

What it is

You drive to the customer's home or workplace and clean their car where it is parked: wash, vacuum, wipe down, glass, wheels and a wax or sealant.

  • Hand wash and dry the outside
  • Vacuum and wipe down the inside
  • Clean glass and add wax or sealant

Who it's for

People who like practical, physical work, notice small details and are happy to chat with customers. A car and a few basics are enough to start.

  • You like hands-on, tidy work
  • You have a car that can carry supplies
  • You are patient with details

A normal week

A few jobs to start, plus time for booking, driving, setup and cleanup. Rain, heat and cold can change your plans.

  • Answer quote requests and book jobs
  • Drive, clean one or two cars, pack up
  • Post photos and follow up with customers

What it costs to start

A starter kit, a decent vacuum, a plan for water and power, and insurance. Costs vary by place, so price your own list before you buy.

  • Buckets, mitts, microfiber, products
  • Vacuum and a water and power plan
  • Insurance and any local registration

Tools you'll use

Simple hand tools and a phone. You book, quote and take payment from your phone, and clean with a small, well-chosen kit.

  • Two buckets with grit guards
  • Plush microfiber and soft brushes
  • Phone for photos, quotes and payments

The hard parts

It is tiring work, the start is slow, and dirty cars take longer than you expect. Water rules and weather can block a day.

  • Water and runoff rules vary by place
  • Weather and seasons change your calendar
  • Scratch claims and difficult customers

Lesson 1 of 24 · 3 min read

What a mobile detailer actually does all day

Mobile detailing means you drive to the customer and clean their car right where it is parked, at home or at work. You bring your own supplies and do the job by hand: wash the outside, vacuum and wipe down the inside, clean the glass and wheels, and add a coat of wax or sealant. A light polish can come later, once you have practiced.

People pay for this because it saves them time. They skip the line at the car wash, and they get more care than a machine gives. You are selling a clean car and an easier week, not just soap and towels.

A normal job takes a few hours, depending on the car and how dirty it is. Your day is part cleaning, part driving, part talking to customers and part loading and unloading. It is physical work. You will kneel, bend, scrub and carry water, and feel the weather. A rainy week can empty your calendar.

The hard parts are real:

  1. The start is slow, because nobody knows you yet.
  2. Dirty cars take longer than they look. Pet hair, sand and spilled drinks can double a job.
  3. Water rules and permission can decide where you are allowed to work. The next guide covers this.
  4. The car belongs to someone else, so care matters more than speed.

The good news is that you can start small. A modest set of supplies, a vehicle that can carry them and some practice can get you to your first paying job. Costs vary by place, so price your own list before you buy anything.

By the end of this first module you will understand the work, know what to check where you live, and have a simple menu for your first customer.

Your step

Write down the last three times you paid for a car wash or detail, and what you liked and disliked about each one.

Lesson 2 of 24 · 1 min read

Wash only where washing is allowed

Soapy water that runs into a storm drain often flows to a creek or river without being cleaned. Because of that, many cities and counties limit outdoor car washing, and some add extra rules for people who wash cars as a business. The rules differ by country, state and city, so check your local rules instead of guessing. Your city or county website is the place to start.

Then there is permission. Many apartment buildings, condo associations and workplaces do not allow washing on site, even when the city does. Ask the owner or manager first, and get the answer in writing, even if it is a short email.

If water is the problem, you can still work. Rinseless and waterless products use very little water, and you will learn them later in this course.

Your step

Search your city or county name plus "washing vehicles runoff" and write down what the official page says about washing at home and for business.

Lesson 3 of 24 · 3 min read

Build a three-package menu you can finish on time

Beginners often offer everything. That confuses customers and turns jobs into all-day battles. Start with three packages and nothing else.

Here is one example menu. Change it to fit your area and your skills.

  1. Express Wash. Hand wash and dry, wheels and tires, and outside windows. Good for a car that is already fairly clean.
  2. Interior Clean. Vacuum, wipe down surfaces, and clean the inside glass and door jambs. Good for family cars and messy back seats.
  3. Full Detail. Everything in the first two, plus a coat of wax or sealant and a careful check at the end.

Why only three? A short menu makes the choice easy, and most customers want to pick quickly and trust you. It also protects you, because you only sell what you can do well with the tools you own.

Leave the advanced work off for now. Ceramic coating and heavy paint correction take real training, and a mistake can damage someone's paint. You will see in module four why it is worth learning properly before you sell it.

Time your packages on your own car or a friend's before you promise anything. Write down how long each one really takes, including setup and packing up. If an Interior Clean takes you two hours, tell customers two hours, not the one hour you hoped for. Almost every beginner is slower than expected, and that is normal.

Add-ons can come later, such as pet hair removal, headlight cleaning or a clay bar treatment. Keep them on a separate line so the main menu stays easy to read. You will put prices on everything in module four, so for now just get the shape right.

Your step

Write your three packages on one page with a name, a list of what is included, and the time you expect each to take on a mid-size car.

Lesson 4 of 24 · 1 min read

Write what each package includes, and what it does not

Most complaints come from a mismatch: the customer expected one thing and got another. A clear description of each package prevents it. For every package, write a short list of what you do in plain words, such as "vacuum seats, floors and trunk" or "hand wash and dry the whole outside".

Then write what you do not do. Common examples are deep stain removal, heavy pet hair, mold, odor removal, scratch repair, engine bays and the underside of the car. Say that very dirty cars cost more, and that you will look at photos first. You will learn how to quote from photos in module three.

Put these lines on your price list, in your booking messages and in your agreement. When customers read them up front, a surprise on the day becomes rare.

Your step

Under each package, add one line starting with "Does not include" and name at least two things, such as deep stain removal or engine bays.

Lesson 5 of 24 · 3 min read

Set up the basics: name, registration and insurance

You can set up a small detailing business quickly, but do it properly. Rules differ by country, state and city, so treat this guide as a list of questions to answer, not as legal advice.

Start with a name. Pick something short that tells people what you do, and check that nobody nearby already uses it. Then look up how small businesses are registered where you live. In the US, many people start as a sole proprietor, which is the simplest setup, and others form an LLC. The right choice depends on your situation, so ask a qualified person if you are unsure. Your state, county or city may also want a business license, and some places have extra rules for mobile businesses. Your local government websites are the best place to look, and a nearby small business development center may offer free advice.

Next, open a separate bank account for the business, even a basic one. It keeps your records clean and makes tax time much easier. You will use it again in module four.

Then talk to an insurance agent. You will be working on cars that belong to other people, so ask about liability coverage, which can help if you damage something or someone gets hurt. Also ask whether damage to a customer's car while it is in your care is covered. Policies differ a lot, so ask what is covered, what is not, what it costs and how to make a claim. Some workplaces and apartment managers will want to see proof of insurance before they let you work.

Write down every answer in one place. You will come back to it often.

Your step

Look up your city or county business license page, and email one insurance agent to ask what coverage a mobile detailer should have.

Lesson 6 of 24 · 1 min read

Do a full practice detail on your own car first

Before a stranger pays you, clean a car that you or your family own. Do the Full Detail from start to finish: wheels, wash, dry, interior, glass and sealant. Use only the tools and products you plan to carry, so you learn what really works for you.

Time each stage on your phone. Those numbers become your real times, and later your prices. Note how long you spend hunting for things in your trunk, too.

Take before and after photos. Look at the results in daylight and from different angles, then list what you missed. Streaky glass and skipped door jambs are common first-time misses.

Ask a friend to judge it honestly, then repeat on a second car if you can. Two practice rounds teach you more than ten videos.

Your step

Detail your own or a family member's car this week, using your three-package steps, and time every stage on your phone.
