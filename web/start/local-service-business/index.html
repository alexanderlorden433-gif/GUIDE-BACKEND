Lesson 1 of 49 · 3 min read

Pick one service and a tight service area

Start with one clear service, not a menu of everything you could do. "House cleaning for busy families on the west side" is easy to remember and easy to pass on. "Cleaning, yard work, handyman, whatever you need" is not. When a happy customer tells a neighbor about you, they need to describe you in one sentence, and a vague offer makes that hard.

Pick something people near you already pay for, that you can do well today with tools you have or can get cheaply. Residential cleaning, lawn care, small home repairs, pressure washing and mobile car detailing are all common starting points. Then write your service as one plain line, like "Weekly lawn mowing and edging for homes in Oak Park."

Next, draw your service area. Open a map, mark your home, and circle what you can reach in roughly 15 to 20 minutes, or list the five or six neighborhoods you want to work in. A tight area keeps drive time down, and drive time is time nobody pays you for. It also lets you aim your flyers, local groups and online profile at the same few streets, so people start seeing your name more than once.

Start narrow on both, and widen later. Adding a second service or a new neighborhood once you're busy is easy. Trying to narrow down after months of taking any job, anywhere, is much harder, because your reputation is scattered and half your week is spent in the car.

Your step

Write your service as one plain sentence and list the five or six neighborhoods you'll serve first.

Lesson 2 of 49 · 1 min read

Say no to jobs outside your skills

When you're starting out, every inquiry feels like money you can't afford to lose. But taking a job you don't really know how to do, like a house cleaner agreeing to fix a leaking faucet, is how you end up with a bad review, a free redo and a customer who never calls again.

Keep a short list of trusted pros in nearby trades and pass that work to them. Practice one line until it feels natural: "That's not my specialty, but I know someone great who can help." The customer still remembers you as helpful, and those pros may start sending work back your way.

Your step

Write down three trusted local pros in nearby trades you can refer jobs to, with their phone numbers.

Lesson 3 of 49 · 3 min read

Get licensed, insured and legally set up

Before you take your first paid job, find out what your area requires for your type of work. The rules vary a lot by location and by trade. A dog walker and an electrician face very different requirements, but many service businesses need some mix of a business license, liability insurance and, for certain trades, a specific certification or permit.

Your local government's small business office or website is the place to start. Search for your city or county name plus "business license," or call and describe exactly what you'll be doing. If the answer is unclear, or you work in a regulated trade, a short consultation with a local business attorney or accountant is money well spent.

Then get general liability insurance. It covers claims if you damage someone's property or someone gets hurt during a job, like a ladder scratching a hardwood floor or a customer slipping on a floor you just mopped. Many commercial clients, property managers, referral networks and booking platforms won't work with you at all without proof of it, so it opens doors as well as protecting you.

Keep the setup simple:

  • Ask your local small business office what your service needs
  • Get liability insurance quotes from two or three providers
  • Save every license, policy and renewal date in one folder

Sorting this out now costs far less than finding out months later that you've been operating without something you needed. It also gives you an easy, confident answer when a customer asks, "Are you licensed and insured?"

Your step

Call or visit your local small business office this week and ask exactly which licenses and permits your service needs.

Lesson 4 of 49 · 1 min read

Keep your liability insurance current

Insurance only protects you if it's active on the day something goes wrong. A cracked window, a damaged floor or a customer who trips over your equipment can lead to repair bills or claims bigger than a whole year of profit. If your policy quietly lapsed last month, that bill is yours.

Put your renewal date in your calendar with a two-week warning, so you have time to compare quotes or sort out a payment problem. Once a year, check that your coverage still matches what you actually do. If you started with lawn mowing and now also trim trees or pressure wash, tell your insurer, because a claim from a service your policy doesn't cover may not be paid.

Your step

Add your insurance renewal date to your calendar now, with a reminder two weeks before it's due.

Lesson 5 of 49 · 1 min read

Recheck licensing rules once a year

Licensing rules aren't set once and forgotten. Cities and counties update their requirements, new permits appear for certain kinds of work, and adding a service can put you under a different rule. Working without a license you need can lead to fines, trouble with your insurance, or customers refusing to pay, even if you never knew about the change.

Once a year, check your local government's rules for each service you offer. Doing it in the same month you renew your insurance makes it easy to remember. Keep copies of every license and permit, with its renewal date, in one folder, paper or digital. And when you're about to add a new service, check the rules before you advertise it, not after the first job.

Your step

Create one folder for your licenses and permits, and set a yearly reminder to recheck your local rules.

Lesson 6 of 49 · 1 min read

Set money aside for taxes and new gear

Tax bills and broken equipment never arrive at a convenient time. When the mower dies in peak season or a tax payment comes due, a business with no savings can stall for weeks or end up running on a credit card.

Open a separate savings account just for this. Every time a customer pays you, move a fixed share of that payment into it right away, before you spend anything. Treat it as money that was never yours to spend. Part of it covers taxes, and part builds a fund to replace the tools and equipment you're wearing out every week.

How much to set aside depends on where you live and how your business is set up, so ask a tax professional what share makes sense for you. Then make the transfer the same day, every time, or automate it.

Your step

Open a separate savings account and ask a tax professional what share of each payment to move into it.
