Module 1: Start with money you can lose

The whole first module, free. Each lesson ends with one thing to do, so you finish with real progress, not just notes.

Lesson 1 of 49 · 3 min read

Work out what you can afford to lose

Before you compare strategies, work out one number: the amount you could lose completely without changing how you live. Not the amount you'd be annoyed to lose. The amount that, if it went to zero tomorrow, would still leave your rent paid, your bills covered and your savings plan intact.

That number is the honest size of your trading money, and it should decide how much goes into trading versus long-term saving. Plenty of people do this backwards. They find a strategy that looks promising, work out how much it would take to make the returns interesting, and fund that instead. The strategy's upside sets the deposit, and the deposit quietly becomes money they can't actually afford to lose.

To get to your own number, write down your monthly income, your fixed bills, what you put aside for savings, and any big cost coming in the next year. What's genuinely spare after all of that is the pool you can risk. If the answer is small, that's useful information rather than a reason to stretch.

Borrowed money changes this calculation completely. Trading on margin means you control a bigger position than your cash alone would allow, so losses grow at the same rate as gains, and they can run past what you put in. For someone still learning, that turns a manageable mistake into a serious one.

Your number isn't permanent either. It should move when your life moves, which is the last lesson in this module.

One thing to be clear about: this is education, not personal financial advice. How much risk suits you depends on circumstances only you and a licensed financial professional can see properly. If real money is going in, that conversation is worth having first.

Your step

Write down your income, bills, savings and big upcoming costs, then set the one amount you could lose without it changing your life.

Lesson 2 of 49 · 1 min read

Only fund an account with spare money

Money you need for rent or bills behaves differently once it's in a trading account. Every normal dip feels like an emergency, because for you it is one, and people in that state sell at the worst possible moment. The market didn't do anything unusual. The pressure came from where the money came from.

Fund the account from what's genuinely spare. Before you transfer anything, list next month's bills, this year's savings goals, and any cost you already know is coming. Move over what's left after that, and nothing else.

Then do one more thing. Assume the whole amount could go to zero. If that thought makes you want to transfer less, transfer less. That reaction is good information.

Your step

Before your next transfer, list next month's bills and savings goals and move over only what is genuinely left.

Lesson 3 of 49 · 1 min read

Fill an emergency fund before you trade

Without savings sitting outside your trading account, a broken boiler or a medical bill turns into a forced sale. You don't get to choose the timing. You sell whatever you can, at whatever price the market happens to be offering that week, and you take the loss because the plumber needs paying.

An emergency fund removes that link. The target most people work toward is a few months of basic living costs, held in a regular savings account where it's boring and instantly available. Not invested. Not at your broker. Just sitting there.

Fill that first. Once it's full, money can start flowing to trading, and a surprise cost stops being a trading decision at all.

Your step

Open a separate savings account and set a monthly transfer until it holds a few months of basic living costs.

Lesson 4 of 49 · 1 min read

Keep trading money in its own account

When trading money sits in the same account as your spending, two things go wrong. You can't see your real results, because a salary landing and a grocery shop drown out what your trades actually did. And after a loss, topping up is one tap away, so you add money without ever really deciding to.

Open a separate account used only for trading, and fund it with a set amount on a set schedule. Monthly is fine. Weekly is fine. What matters is that the amount was decided in advance, not in the hour after a bad trade.

Then keep one rule: no mid-month top-ups from your spending money. If the account is down, it stays down until the next scheduled transfer. That rule is what turns a number on a screen into a real limit.

Your step

Open a dedicated trading account this week and set one scheduled transfer amount you will not top up between dates.

Lesson 5 of 49 · 3 min read

Set your risk rules before your strategy

New traders spend their first months hunting for the right strategy and almost no time on how much they put at risk per trade. That's backwards. An average strategy with tight risk control can survive a bad month. A brilliant strategy with no risk control can't survive one bad week.

Risk management comes down to two decisions, and both get made before you enter anything.

The first is position size. A common general principle is to risk only a small percentage of your total trading capital on any single trade, so a run of losses is annoying rather than fatal. Work it out as an amount of money, not a feeling. If your trading pot is $2,000 and you cap risk at one percent, that's $20 at risk on the trade, and your position size follows from where the stop sits.

The second is the stop. Decide the price at which you're wrong before you enter, and place that order straight away. Stops that get moved mid-trade are usually being moved to avoid admitting a loss, and that's how a small planned loss becomes a large unplanned one.

Write both numbers next to every trade: the amount you're risking, and the price that ends it. If you can't fill those in, you don't have a trade yet. You have an opinion.

Losses are a normal part of this, even when you do everything right. The point of risk rules isn't to avoid them. It's to make sure no single loss, and no run of losses, takes you out of the market or out of your own finances.

As with everything here, this is general education rather than personal advice. What size suits you depends on your circumstances, and a licensed financial professional can help you judge that.

Your step

Pick your maximum risk per trade as a cash amount and write it, with a stop price, next to your next planned trade.

Lesson 6 of 49 · 1 min read

Aim for small steady gains, not jackpots

Chasing one huge win changes your behavior long before it changes your results. A big target needs a big position, and a big position needs you to be right. That's how a single trade ends up carrying months of careful progress on its back.

The alternative is boring on purpose. Set a modest target for each trade and a fixed position size you won't exceed, whatever the setup looks like. Small repeatable results add up in a way a lottery ticket never does, and they don't ask you to be right about anything dramatic.

Change how you score yourself too. At the end of a trade, the question isn't whether you made money. It's whether you followed the plan you wrote. Good execution with a losing result is still a good trade, and a reckless win is still a problem.

Your step

Write a modest target and a fixed maximum position size, then score your next five trades on execution only.

Lesson 7 of 49 · 1 min read

Check trading against your real money goals

It's easy to spend a year deep in individual trades and never ask the bigger question: is this moving me toward anything? A deposit on a home, debt cleared, retirement savings on track. Those are what the money is for, and they don't show up on a chart.

Every few months, write down your top three money goals in plain words, with rough numbers and dates. Then put your trading next to them. How much time did it take? What did it contribute after fees? Is it ahead of, behind, or roughly level with simply holding a broad market fund?

If the answer is uncomfortable, that isn't a failure. It's a signal to adjust something: less risk, fewer hours, a different split between trading and long-term saving. The goals get to overrule the hobby.

Your step

Write your top three money goals with rough numbers and dates, then compare your last three months of trading against them.

Lesson 8 of 49 · 1 min read

Reset your risk after a big life change

Your risk limits were set for the life you had when you wrote them. A new baby, a job loss, a house purchase or a parent who needs help all change how much loss you can absorb, even though your strategy hasn't changed a line.

The mistake is leaving the old numbers in place because the trading is going fine. Your capacity for loss isn't about how well your system works. It's about what else that money is holding up.

After any big change, rewrite two numbers: your maximum loss per trade, and the total you have at risk across the account. Lower them first, by default. You can raise them again once life feels settled and the new bills are predictable, and nothing is lost by trading small for a few months.

Your step

After your next big life change, rewrite your maximum loss per trade and your total account risk, both lower than before.
