How affiliate marketing actually works

Affiliate marketing means recommending a product with a special link and earning a cut when someone buys through it. You don't stock anything, ship anything or answer customer complaints. Your job is to help the right person find the right product, honestly.

Here's how the money moves. A company, called the merchant, runs an affiliate program. You join it and get a tracking link with your ID built in. When someone clicks your link and buys within a set window, called the cookie duration, the sale is credited to you. That window depends on the program. It can be as short as a day or last a month or more. The tracking happens automatically, and you can see your clicks and sales in the program's dashboard.

Why would a company share its money? Because you're cheaper than ads. An ad costs money for every click, whether anyone buys or not. You only get paid when a sale actually happens, which makes you a sales team that's paid purely on results.

Commissions vary a lot depending on what you promote:

  • Physical products usually pay a small percentage, and Amazon sits at the low end
  • Digital products like courses and software tend to pay a bigger share
  • Some subscription tools pay you every month the customer stays
  • Premium services, like web hosting, often pay a flat amount per signup

To start, you need three things: a place to post, like a blog, TikTok, YouTube or an email list, a group of people who listen, even a small one, and products you'd recommend for free. Trust matters more than follower count. A few hundred people who value your opinion can be worth more than a huge audience that scrolls straight past. And because you're paid for these recommendations, you'll tell your audience every time. You'll learn exactly how later in this module.

Your step

List 20 products, apps or tools you've used for at least a month, then star the five you'd recommend to a friend for free.

Choose your first affiliate programs

The easiest recommendations come from products you already use. Make a list of every app, tool and product you pay for or reach for every week. Then search each name plus the words "affiliate program". Many brands run one, and the signup page usually lists the commission and the main rules.

Most beginners also join Amazon Associates, because you can link to almost anything on Amazon and shoppers already trust it. The trade-off is low commissions and a short cookie window. Beyond Amazon there are two kinds of programs. Affiliate networks like Awin, CJ and Impact put many brands in one dashboard. Direct programs are run by the company itself. A sensible start is one network plus a few direct programs for products you love. Aim for five to eight programs you can promote well, not fifty you'll never mention.

Before you join any program, check:

  • The commission, and whether it's paid once or every month
  • The cookie window, since a longer one gives buyers more time to decide
  • The payout threshold and the payment methods it supports
  • Its rules on where and how you're allowed to share links

Then walk away from anything with red flags: a program that charges you to join, commissions that look too good to be true, no clear dashboard to track your sales, or a product with poor reviews and lots of refunds. Your reputation is the whole business, and one bad product can damage it.

The simplest test is this. If you wouldn't recommend the product with no commission at all, don't recommend it with one.

Your step

Pick five products from your list, look up their affiliate programs, and note each one's commission, cookie window and link rules.

Lesson 3 of 24 · 1 min read

Only recommend what you've used or researched

Your audience clicks your links because they trust your judgment. Recommend one product that breaks, disappoints or turns out to be a scam, and they'll start doubting every link you share after it. One bad pick can undo the trust that many good ones built.

Before you promote anything, use it yourself for a few weeks. If you can't, research it properly: read the negative reviews and the refund policy, not just the sales page. Write down two honest drawbacks and put them in your content. Naming the downsides shows people you're on their side, and it helps the wrong buyers skip it.

Your step

Pick one product you plan to promote and write down two honest drawbacks you'll mention alongside it.

Lesson 4 of 24 · 3 min read

Tell your audience you earn a commission

If you earn money from a recommendation, you have to tell your audience. In the US, the Federal Trade Commission requires a clear and conspicuous disclosure of any material connection with a brand, and an affiliate commission counts. Many other countries have similar rules, so check the ones where you live. Breaking them can bring penalties, and it can cost you your audience's trust.

Clear means plain words. "I earn a commission if you buy through my links" is clear. "Affiliate link" is usually clear enough. "Thanks to the brand" is too vague, because it never says you're paid. Use "sponsored" only when a brand actually paid you for the post.

Conspicuous means people see it before they click:

  • On a blog post, put it near the top, above the first link
  • In an Instagram or TikTok caption, put it in the first lines, not buried in hashtags
  • On YouTube, say it out loud early in the video and add it to the description
  • Where a platform has its own disclosure setting, like YouTube's paid promotion box, use it when it applies

Programs add their own rules on top of the law. Amazon requires a specific line on your site, "As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases," and it doesn't allow its links in emails. Read the terms of every program and platform you use. Not knowing a rule won't save your account.

Don't worry that disclosure will scare buyers off. A short, friendly line is enough, and people who know where you stand are more likely to believe what you recommend. Say it plainly, then get on with the useful part.

Your step

Write a one-line disclosure in your own words and add it to your bio, your link page and the top of every post with links.

Lesson 5 of 24 · 1 min read

Put your disclosure before the first link

A disclosure only works if people see it before they click. One line at the bottom of a long post, or tucked under a wall of hashtags, doesn't do the job. Viewers who find out later feel tricked, and platforms and regulators can penalize creators who hide paid relationships.

Make it the first thing near your links. In a blog post, add a plain line above the first link, like "I earn a commission if you buy through my links." In a caption, put it in the opening lines. In a video, say it out loud before you show or name the product, then repeat it in the description.

Your step

Open your last three posts with affiliate links and move the disclosure above the first link in each one.

Lesson 6 of 24 · 3 min read

Five beginner mistakes and how to avoid them

New affiliates tend to stall for the same few reasons. That's good news, because it means you can avoid them from day one.

Watch out for these:

  1. Promoting everything. Joining every program and linking random products leaves you with no focus and no authority. Pick five to eight products you know well and go deep.
  2. Links before audience. Posting links to nobody is like opening a shop on an empty street. Spend your first months publishing useful content and earning attention.
  3. Ignoring search. A social post fades in a day or two, but a blog post or YouTube video that ranks in search can bring visitors for years. Make some of your content searchable.
  4. Not tracking. If you don't know which post drove which sale, you're guessing. Tag your links and check your numbers every week.
  5. Quitting too early. Affiliate income builds slowly, over months rather than weeks.

That last one deserves a moment. The first stretch often feels like a lot of work for very little, because content takes time to find its audience and search rankings take time to build. Treat this like a business with a long runway, not a quick win. Keep publishing, keep tracking, and keep improving what works.

None of these take talent or money to fix, just a bit of discipline. The rest of this course shows you how to do each one well, starting with the programs you'll join next.
