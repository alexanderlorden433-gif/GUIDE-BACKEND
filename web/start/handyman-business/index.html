Is it right for you?

What it is

You do small repairs and installs in customers' homes, like mounting TVs, assembling furniture, patching walls and fixing doors.

  • Mount TVs, shelves and mirrors
  • Patch, paint and caulk small areas
  • Fix doors and swap hardware

Who it's for

People who are handy, tidy and dependable, and who are happy talking with customers. You also need to be fine with physical work.

  • Reliable and on time
  • Comfortable on ladders and on your feet
  • Patient with customers and small jobs

A normal week

You answer inquiries, quote a few jobs, batch the work by area, then invoice and update your books. Weeks can be uneven, especially at the start.

  • Reply to inquiries and book visits
  • Batch jobs by area and by day
  • Invoice, photos and simple books

What it costs to start

Mostly tools, safety gear, a stepladder and insurance, plus any registration or license fees. Costs vary a lot by area, so get local quotes.

  • Starter tools and safety gear
  • Liability insurance and registration
  • A vehicle, fuel and a business phone

Tools you'll use

A cordless drill, level, stud finder and hand tools do most of the work. Phone apps handle photos, scheduling, invoices and payments.

  • Drill, level, stud finder, stepladder
  • Square or Stripe to take payments
  • Google Business Profile and a calendar

The hard parts

Your limits come first: licensed trades, dollar caps and safety. Starts can be slow, the work is physical, and small jobs mean thin margins.

  • Staying inside local license rules
  • A slow start and uneven weeks
  • Physical work and thin margins

Lesson 1 of 24 · 3 min read

What a handyman business really looks like

A handyman business is you, a vehicle full of tools, and a list of small jobs that people don't have the time, the tools or the confidence to do themselves. Think of mounting a TV, hanging shelves, assembling furniture, patching a hole in drywall, fixing a sticking door or re-caulking a tub. Each job is small. Customers pay for you showing up on time, doing it properly and leaving the place clean.

Your customers are busy homeowners, renters, older people who shouldn't be on a ladder, and landlords who need a unit fixed between tenants. Most have a list of little things that has sat there for months. Your job is to clear it.

Know what the work is really like before you start:

  • It is physical. You will lift, kneel, climb and breathe dust.
  • Jobs are small, so travel, set-up and clean-up take a big share of your time.
  • The start is usually slow, because nobody knows you yet, and some weeks will be quiet.
  • Demand moves with the seasons and with moving days.
  • Some customers will push on price or ask for more than they agreed to.

The good news is that you can start with tools you may already own, begin on evenings and weekends, and build a name by being dependable. Reliable is rarer than skilled.

One rule runs through the whole course. The only work worth taking is work you can do well, safely and legally. What a handyman may do differs by country, state and city, so you will check your local rules soon.

The plan from here: pick a short list of jobs, learn which work to turn down, set up legally and simply, then do the work well, find customers and run it like a business.

Your step

Write down the last five small repair or install jobs you did or helped with, and how long each one took.

Lesson 2 of 24 · 1 min read

Start with five jobs you can do well

New handymen often say yes to everything, then end up on jobs they are slow at or nervous about. A narrow list is easier to price, explain and do safely. Start with about five jobs.

Pick them with three questions. Have you done this job more than once? Can you do it with a small set of tools you can afford? Is it normal handyman work where you live, and not something that needs a licensed trade?

Good first candidates for many people are mounting TVs and shelves, assembling furniture, patching small drywall holes, touch-up painting, and swapping door handles and hardware.

Practice any job you are unsure of at your own place or a friend's before you charge anyone. Add new jobs later, one at a time.

Your step

Write your list of five jobs, then mark any you have not done at least twice and plan a practice run at your place or a friend's.

Lesson 3 of 24 · 3 min read

Know where handyman work stops

Handyman work has a boundary, and where it sits depends on where you live. In many places, electrical, plumbing, gas, roofing and structural work can only be done by a licensed tradesperson. Many places also cap the dollar size of a job a handyman may take without a contractor license, and some ask handymen to register or hold a license even below that cap. In other places the rules are lighter. You cannot guess which applies to you, and "my neighbor does it" is not a defense.

Doing licensed work without the license can bring fines, may leave you without insurance cover, and can make you personally responsible if something goes wrong. A wiring fault or a hidden water leak can cause serious damage or hurt someone. No single job is worth that risk.

So check before you advertise anything:

  1. Search for your state or city plus "handyman license" and read the official government page, not a blog.
  2. Find the contractor licensing board and call or email them if the page is unclear.
  3. Ask what counts as licensed work, whether there is a dollar cap per job, and whether the cap includes materials.
  4. Ask whether your city also needs a business license or a permit for the work on your list.
  5. Write down what they say, with the date and who told you.

Permits are a related question. Moving walls, changing plumbing or wiring and similar work often needs one, and that is not handyman territory.

Rules change, so look again once a year. If a job is unclear, treat it as outside your limit until an official source tells you otherwise. When the answer is "that needs a licensed trade", you pass the job on, and the next lesson shows you how.

Your step

Find your local rules page for handyman or contractor work and write down any dollar limit and the list of licensed trades.

Lesson 4 of 24 · 1 min read

Say no to a job and send it on

Saying no is part of the trade, and done well it builds trust. When a request falls outside your list, your limits or your local rules, say so kindly and give a next step.

Try something like this: "Thanks for thinking of me. That job needs a licensed electrician, so I can't take it on. I can patch and paint the wall afterward. Would you like a name to call?"

Keep a short list of two or three licensed people you would happily recommend. If a tradesperson ever pays you for a referral, tell the customer. A good list also sends work back to you, because trades are often asked for small jobs they don't want.

Never take a job you should refuse because the money looks good.

Your step

Write your own "no, but here is what I can do" message and save it on your phone, then ask two licensed tradespeople if you can refer customers to them.

Lesson 5 of 24 · 3 min read

Register your business and get insured

Before your first paid job, set up the basics. The details depend on where you live, so treat this lesson as a list of questions to ask, not a list of answers. Rules differ by country, state and city.

Registration. In many places you can work as a sole proprietor under your own name. If you want a business name, you may need to register it. Some cities or counties want a business license, and some places want a handyman license or registration. Start with your state or city small business website. A local small business office can often explain the steps for free.

A separate bank account. Open a basic account just for the business. Customer payments go in and business costs come out. It keeps your records clean and makes tax time far less painful.

Insurance. Ask a local insurance agent about general liability insurance for handyman work. It is designed to help pay if you damage a customer's property or someone is hurt because of your work. Ask what it covers and excludes, whether it covers every job on your list, and how to get a certificate of insurance. Property managers often ask for one before they hire you. Your tools, vehicle and health may need separate cover, and personal policies may not cover business use.

Taxes. Ask an accountant or your tax authority what to register for and what records to keep. Do not guess.

All of this usually takes a few phone calls and some forms. Do it before the first paid job, not after the first problem.

Your step

Call one insurance agent and ask what general liability cover for a handyman costs and covers where you live. Write down the answer.

Lesson 6 of 24 · 1 min read

Write a job menu a neighbor can say yes to

A job menu is a short list of what you do, so customers know what to ask for. It should fit on one phone screen.

List your five jobs in plain words, such as "Mount a TV", "Hang shelves or a mirror", "Assemble furniture", "Patch a small wall hole" and "Fix a sticking door". Next to each, note what is included. Add a line about your minimum charge for any visit. You will set real numbers in the pricing lessons, and prices vary by area, so check what handymen near you charge.

Then add where you work and what you don't do, such as "No electrical, plumbing or gas work." End with how to reach you.

Keep it honest and short. Send it to three people and ask which job they would book first.

Your step

Write your job menu, including a line on what you don't do, and send it to three people you know.
