Module 1: Pick a product worth selling

The whole first module, free. Each lesson ends with one thing to do, so you finish with real progress, not just notes.

Lesson 1 of 49 · 3 min read

Choose a first product people already want

Your first product decides more about your store than your logo, your theme or your ads. Pick something people are already looking for and everything after gets easier. Pick something nobody searches for and even great marketing has to fight uphill.

Start by looking for signs of real demand. The strongest ones are:

  • Repeat purchases: things people use up and buy again, like skincare, coffee or pet treats
  • Gift appeal: products people happily buy for someone else
  • Searches with weak answers: people are looking for it, but the current listings are generic, poor or hard to find

Next, read the competition honestly. Some competition is a good sign, because it proves people pay for this kind of product. What you want to avoid is a market packed with well-funded brands selling nearly the same item for less than you could. If the first page of results is full of identical products at prices you can't match, keep looking, or find a specific angle they're ignoring, like a size, a style or a type of customer.

Then run the real numbers before you commit. Write down what one sale actually costs you: the product, shipping, packaging, payment fees and an allowance for returns. Say a candle sells for $30. If it costs $9 to make, $7 to ship, $2 to pack, $1.20 in fees, and you set aside $1 per order for returns, you keep about $9.80 before any marketing. That might be fine, or it might be too thin once you start paying for ads.

A product that looks exciting can still lose money once every cost is counted. Do this math on paper now, while changing your mind costs nothing.

Your step

Pick two product ideas and write down the full cost of one sale for each: product, shipping, packaging, fees and returns.

Lesson 2 of 49 · 1 min read

Prove one product before you add more

It's tempting to launch with twenty products so the store looks full. But a big catalog spreads your cash, photos, ad budget and attention across items you know nothing about yet. When sales are slow, you can't tell which part is the problem.

Launch one product instead, or one small set that clearly belongs together. Give it a small, fixed ad budget and watch it for a few weeks: how many people buy, what reviewers say, and whether anyone orders again. When it sells steadily at a profit, add the next product, ideally one the same customers would want. If it doesn't, you've learned that cheaply and can change course.

Your step

Pick the one product you'll launch first, then set a small, fixed test budget and a date to review the results.

Lesson 3 of 49 · 3 min read

Pick a sourcing model that fits your risk

Before you can sell a product, you need a way to get it. There are four common models, and each one trades money, control and risk differently.

  • Manufacturing: you have the product made to your design. You get the most control and usually the best margins, but you pay for stock upfront and you're stuck with it if it doesn't sell.
  • Wholesale: you buy finished products in bulk from a brand or distributor and resell them. It's simpler than manufacturing, but you still carry inventory risk.
  • Dropshipping: a supplier stores and ships each order for you. You hold no stock, but margins are lower and you can't check quality before it reaches the customer.
  • Print-on-demand: your designs are printed onto blank items only when someone orders. Almost no upfront cost, but thin margins and limited control over the finished product.

The pattern is simple. The less money you put in upfront, the less you keep per sale and the less control you have over what the customer actually receives.

That's why many sellers start low-risk and move up. You might test a T-shirt design through print-on-demand, or buy a small wholesale order of 50 units, to see whether people really buy. Once a product sells consistently, larger wholesale orders or your own manufacturing start to make sense, because now you're spending on something with proof behind it.

Whichever model you choose, order a sample before you sell. Hold the product, see how it arrives and note how long delivery took. If you wouldn't be happy receiving it, your customers won't be either.

Your step

Choose the sourcing model you'll start with and order one sample of your product to check its quality and delivery time.

Lesson 4 of 49 · 3 min read

Know your true margin before you buy ads

Revenue tells you how much money came in. It doesn't tell you how much you can afford to spend getting it. For that you need your contribution margin: what's left from one order after every cost of making and delivering that sale.

To work it out, start with your selling price and subtract:

  • The product itself, often called cost of goods
  • Shipping and packaging
  • Payment processing fees
  • An allowance for returns and refunds

Here's an example. You sell a water bottle for $35. It costs you $10, shipping and packaging come to $8, fees are about $1.30, and you set aside $1.50 per order for returns. Your contribution margin is about $14.20. That's the most you could spend to win one order and still break even on it.

That number is the ceiling for your customer acquisition cost, meaning what you pay in marketing to get one sale. Set your real target below it, because the goal is profit, not breaking even. Some stores accept a thinner first order because customers reliably come back, but only once they have repeat-purchase numbers to prove it.

Now you can judge ads properly. A campaign with lots of clicks and impressive revenue can still lose money on every order, and one that looks modest might be your most profitable. Compare what each campaign pays per sale with your ceiling, not with how busy it looks.

Scaling ad spend before this math is solid is how plenty of stores turn what looks like growth into real losses. Work out your margin first, then decide how much to spend.

Your step

Write down your selling price and every cost of one order, then calculate the contribution margin you have left.

Lesson 5 of 49 · 1 min read

Count packaging in your product costs

Boxes, mailers, tissue paper, tape, thank-you cards and stickers are easy to forget when you price a product. They're small on their own, but you pay for them on every single order, and leaving them out makes your margin look healthier than it really is.

Work out a per-order packaging cost. List every item that goes into one shipment and divide each bulk purchase by the number of orders it covers. A pack of 100 mailers for $25 is 25 cents an order, a roll of tape that lasts 200 orders adds a few cents more, and so on. Add it all up, then put that figure into each product's cost in your pricing sheet. If different products use different packaging, work it out per product.

Your step

List every packaging item in one order, work out its cost per order, and add that to each product's cost in your pricing sheet.

Lesson 6 of 49 · 3 min read

Choose where your store will live

There are three main places to sell online. The right one depends on your product and how much technical work you want to take on, not on which option is getting the most hype.

  • Hosted platforms: you pay a monthly fee and the company handles hosting, security, updates and checkout. You pick a theme, add products and start selling.
  • Self-hosted, open-source platforms: the software is free or cheap and you get more control over how everything works. In return, you or someone you pay looks after hosting, updates, security and fixes.
  • Marketplaces: large sites with shoppers already browsing. You list your products on their site, but you follow their rules and the customer relationship mostly belongs to them.

For most beginners, a hosted platform is the sensible start. You give up some flexibility, but you can have a working store in days instead of weeks, and your time goes into testing the product instead of maintaining software. While you're still finding out whether people want what you sell, that's a good trade.

Marketplaces are a useful way to test demand quickly, because the traffic is already there. The trade-off is that fees are usually higher, you sit right next to similar listings, and you often can't email buyers or build a direct relationship with them. Some sellers use a marketplace to prove a product sells, then build their own store so they own the customer list.

Before you decide, add up the real monthly cost of each option you're considering: subscription, transaction fees, any paid apps or themes you'd need, and marketplace referral fees. Then pick the one that gets you selling fastest at a cost your margin can carry.

Your step

List the monthly fees, transaction fees and needed add-ons for two platforms you're considering, then choose one.

Lesson 7 of 49 · 1 min read

Watch marketplace fees as closely as ads

Selling on a marketplace usually means several fees on each sale: a listing fee, a referral cut of the price, fulfillment or storage fees if they ship for you, and often their own ads on top. Each one looks small. Stacked together with your ad spend, they can quietly erase the profit on a product that seems to be selling well.

Once a month, download your fee report and work out total fees as a share of sales for each product. A $25 item that loses $9 to fees and $6 to ads has far less room than its price suggests. Where fees eat most of the margin, raise the price, change how it's shipped or stored, or stop selling that item there.

Your step

Download last month's fee report and work out total fees as a share of sales for each product you sell on a marketplace.
